The loop underneath every improvement

Gerber describes business improvement as three separate activities running in a circle, and the names are worth learning properly, because they stop you skipping the middle one.

Innovation is trying a different way of doing something you already do. Not creativity, which thinks up new things, but innovation, which actually does new things: a different opening sentence, a different order of pages, or a different price.

Quantification is measuring what actually happened afterward. This is the step that everybody skips, and skipping it means you end up with opinions instead of knowledge.

Orchestration is making the winner into the rule. Once a change has been proven better, it stops being a choice and becomes the way things are done, written into the manual from Module 12.

Most people do that first step endlessly and never touch the other two. They change things constantly, feel extremely busy, and cannot tell you whether anything actually improved.

Gerber's own exampleA retailer tested salespeople greeting customers with "may I help you?" against a different opening line, and measured the difference in sales.

The point of that story is not the greeting itself. It is that somebody actually counted the difference, and then everybody used the winner, which is the whole method in one sentence.

The five numbers

You do not need a dashboard and you certainly do not need software. Five numbers, written down once a week, will tell you more about this business than any amount of quiet reflection.

One: conversations had. How many actual business owners you spoke to this week about their website, meaning conversations rather than emails sent.

Two: demos shown. How many of those owners actually saw a site that you had already built for them.

Three: quotes given. How many of them received real prices from you, in writing.

Four: jobs won. How many of those quotes turned into a yes and an actual deposit.

Five: monthly recurring income. The total of every care plan currently being paid to you, which is the single number deciding whether this business survives.

That is five numbers and about five minutes, once a week. It is the entire measurement system and it is genuinely enough.

What the numbers tell you

The value here is not in any single number at all, it is in the ratios between them, because every gap points toward a different problem.

Conversations low. This is both the most common failure and the simplest one, and it means you are not leaving the house. Nothing else can be diagnosed until that number reaches at least five a week.

Conversations high, demos low. You are talking to people but not building anything first, so go back to Module 21 and build before you visit.

Demos high, quotes low. You are showing the work and then never naming a price, which is simply nerves, and Module 22 has the sentences for it.

Quotes high, jobs low. Either your price is wrong for the people you are quoting, or you are quoting entirely the wrong people. Check the lane you chose before you start cutting the price.

Jobs won but recurring flat. You are selling builds and not care plans, and this is the expensive failure, because it means rebuilding your income from zero every single month.

Notice that four of those five diagnoses point at something other than your building skill, and that is the honest shape of this business.

One change at a time

The rule that makes any of this measurement work is deeply unglamorous: change one thing, and then wait.

If you change your opening sentence, your price, and your target trade all in the same week, and then results improve, you have learned nothing at all. You cannot tell which change did it, which means you cannot repeat it.

Give every change enough attempts to actually mean something. For a sales script that means ten conversations rather than two, and for a price it means five quotes. Two data points are a coincidence.

The number that lies to youThe number that lies to you is website visits. It feels like the obvious thing to watch, and it tells a small local business almost nothing at all.

Two hundred visits with no orders is worse than twenty visits with three orders, and yet the first one feels considerably better. Count the things that end in money instead: orders placed, bookings made, and calls received.

What to count for the client, too

Your clients need this every bit as much as you do, and giving it to them is most of what a care plan is actually worth.

There are three numbers worth keeping for each client, checked once a month:

  • Orders or bookings taken through the site, which is the number that quietly justifies your entire fee.
  • Calls or direction taps from their Google listing, which is usually free for you to look up.
  • Anything at all that broke during the month, and how quickly you managed to fix it.

Send those three numbers in a short message every quarter. It takes about fifteen minutes and it is the single best defense against a client wondering out loud what they are paying you for.

That quarterly note is exactly why the care plan section of Module 14 includes it, because a plan the client can see working does not get cancelled in March.

Without the sheet in front of you, that week feels much like all the others and you find out two months later.

Making the winner the rule

The last step is the one that genuinely compounds over time, and it takes about thirty seconds.

When something has been proven better, write it into the manual and then stop deciding about it. If the opening line about the nine o'clock customer closed better than the one about design, then that is now simply the opening line, and you do not weigh it up again each time.

This is what Gerber means by removing choice at the operating level of a business, and it sounds cold until you have actually done it. Deciding fresh every single time is exhausting, and it produces worse results than a rule that was tested once and then written down.

Your manual should therefore change slowly and never randomly. Something goes into it because it beat the alternative in a real test, rather than because you had a feeling about it on a Tuesday.

Do this before you finish this part

Make the sheet with its six columns, and then fill in this week honestly, even if most of the row is zero.

Then pick one single thing to test over the next ten conversations, and write down what result would count as better. One thing, ten attempts, and one written definition of success.

Put a repeating reminder in your phone for Friday afternoon to fill in the row, because the habit itself is worth more than any single number sitting in it.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. Name the five numbers you track weekly.

Conversations had, demos shown, quotes given, jobs won, and monthly recurring income. Five minutes once a week, and the last one is what decides whether the business survives.

2. Your demos are high and your quotes are low. What does that mean?

3. Which number lies to you, and what should you count instead?
