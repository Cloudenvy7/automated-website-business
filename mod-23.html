Test small before you bet big

Lean Startup thinking, translated for somebody with rent due and no investors.

EntrepreneurFog index 8.866 min read

The idea, without the startup language

Eric Ries wrote a book called The Lean Startup about companies burning years building things nobody wanted, and his fix was to test the risky assumption early and cheaply, before committing everything to it.

The language around that idea got fashionable and more than a little silly, so here it is stripped back down. Find the thing you are assuming, and then check it in the smallest way possible before you bet anything on it.

That matters considerably more for you than it does for a funded startup, because a startup that guesses wrong is losing investors' money. You would be losing months that you cannot get back.

What you are actually assuming

Every plan in this book rests on a set of assumptions, and it is worth naming your own ones out loud.

  • That local businesses in your lane will pay for this at all.
  • That they will pay your price rather than half of it.
  • That they will keep paying monthly rather than cancelling in March.
  • That you can find enough of them within a reasonable distance.
  • That you can deliver it without it eating your whole week.

Some of those are nearly certain and some are genuinely risky. The riskiest one for almost everybody is the third, because monthly income is the thing the whole business depends on and it is also the thing nobody tests before committing.

Proof and compliments are different things

This distinction is the single most useful thing in the module, so please read it twice.

A compliment is somebody saying they like it. "That looks great." "I would definitely use that." "Send me the details." None of those cost the person anything, and none of them are evidence.

Proof is somebody giving up something. Money, obviously. But also a real appointment on their calendar, their actual menu file, a signed scope paragraph, or an introduction to another owner by name.

The test for whether any feedback is real is very simple. Did it cost them anything at all? If it did not, then be polite about it and write it down as nothing.

The most expensive sentence in small business "Everybody I showed it to loved it."

Of course they did, and people are kind, especially face to face, and especially to somebody trying. Enthusiasm from people who have not paid anything is the most reliable way to talk yourself into a bad plan.

The smallest useful test

You do not need a launch to test something, and you need one real interaction that costs the other person something.

Here are five tests that you could run this week, and each one answers a specific assumption.

AssumptionThe testWhat counts as proof
They will pay at allBuild one demo, show three owners, quote a real priceOne deposit
They will pay my priceQuote the middle package to five owners without discountingTwo say yes at full price
They will pay monthlyOffer a care plan to your first build clientOne recurring payment set up
There are enough of themCount the trade within a fifteen-minute driveForty or more businesses
I can deliver itTime your third build honestly, start to finishUnder six hours including revisions

None of those require a website of your own, a brand, or a registered company, and every one of them can be done inside a fortnight.

What to do with the answer

The entire point of running a test is that you act on the result afterward, and there are only three honest responses available.

It worked, so do more of it. Two owners paid full price, so stop redesigning your offer and go and find eight more owners.

It failed for a fixable reason. Nobody bought, and all three said the same thing about the price or the timing. Change that one thing and run it again.

It failed for a structural reason. There are only nine businesses of that type near you, so no amount of persistence fixes it. Change the lane rather than trying harder.

That third outcome is the one people refuse to accept, and it is the cheapest possible thing to learn early. Finding out in week two that your lane is too small costs you two weeks. Finding out in month eight costs you the year.

Testing a price change without losing the customer

This particular situation comes up constantly, so it deserves a method of its own.

You suspect your price is too low, which it probably is, and you do not want to test it by raising prices on existing clients and losing them.

So test it on new customers only, exactly as Module 14 described. Quote the higher number to the next five people and count.

Read the result properly, because this trips people up. If all five say yes immediately, your price is still too low and you should raise it again. If three say yes, you have found roughly the right number. If none say yes and they all name the price, you went too far this time.

Nobody objecting to your price at all is not a good sign, it is a piece of information that you have been quietly ignoring.

Do not test everything

A warning belongs here, because this particular method can very easily become an excuse.

Testing is for the assumptions that are both risky and expensive to get wrong. It is not for the color of your invoice, the name of your business, or whether your logo should be blue.

Those decisions do not need evidence at all, they need to be made quickly so that you can get back to the only two things that matter, which are talking to owners and delivering the work.

If you find yourself designing a test for something small, you are probably avoiding a conversation with a stranger. Most testing paralysis is fear wearing a lab coat.

Do this before the next module

Pick out the single assumption that would hurt most if it turned out to be wrong. For most readers that is either the monthly payment or the size of the lane they chose.

Design one test for it, and keep it small enough to finish inside two weeks. Write down three things before you start: what you are testing, what result would count as proof, and what you will do if it fails.

Writing the third one in advance is what stops you from explaining away a bad result afterward, which everybody does when they have not decided beforehand.
