Part 1 — Ground floor · Module 01

What this business actually is

The plain-English version of "automated website development," and the four different ways it puts money in your pocket.

MarketFog index 9.127 min read

Start with the sentence

Every business you have ever admired can be explained in a single sentence, and if you cannot say yours out loud, then you do not have a business yet. What you have instead is an interest.

Here is the sentence for this one:

You build websites for local businesses using artificial intelligence, and then you charge those businesses every month to keep the websites working.

Read that twice, because both halves matter here. The first half is the part everybody notices, while the second half is the part that quietly pays your rent in December.

Most people who try this business only ever hear the first half. They learn to build a site and sell it once for a few hundred dollars, and then they go looking for the next stranger to sell to. That is not a business, that is a long series of small emergencies.

What the customer thinks they are buying

A restaurant owner does not actually want a website, and in truth nobody has ever wanted a website. What that owner wants is for a hungry person at 6:40 on a Tuesday evening to find them, believe them, and place an order.

The website is only the machine that produces that result, which means you are selling the result and not the machine.

This sounds like word games until the moment you try to charge money, and then it becomes the whole ballgame. A website is a thing, and any customer can compare your thing against a fifteen-dollar template they saw online. A result is different, because a result has a value the owner can feel in the register at the end of the night.

Say it this way

Wrong: "I can build you a website for four hundred dollars."

Right: "Right now somebody nearby searches for chicken and waffles, and they find your competitor instead of you. I can fix that, and then I can keep it fixed."

The four ways money comes in

Almost nobody maps this out before they start, which is a shame, because there are four separate income lines in this business and they each behave in very different ways.

1. The build fee

This is the one-time payment for creating the site. In 2026 most small shops charge between one thousand and three thousand dollars for a straightforward site, while businesses leaning on artificial intelligence often charge a good deal less. Roughly a quarter of them now quote under five hundred dollars.

That range is enormous, and where you land inside it has almost nothing to do with your technical skill. It has everything to do with who you sell to and how well you explain the value to them.

The build fee feels like the whole business when you are starting out, but it is actually the smallest and least reliable of the four income lines.

2. The monthly care plan

This is money that arrives whether or not you found a new customer this month. You keep the site running, update the hours, swap out the specials, fix things when they break, and stay reachable when the owner calls you.

Across the industry, most shops selling these plans charge between five hundred and fifteen hundred dollars a month. A useful rule of thumb is that yearly maintenance runs about fifteen to twenty percent of whatever the build cost.

Here is the number that should stop you cold. Shops with no monthly income lose money about fifty-eight percent of the time, while shops where a quarter of income arrives monthly lose money less than ten percent of the time.

Same skill and same tools produce a completely different outcome, and the only real difference is whether money arrives on a schedule or arrives on a prayer.

The trap almost everyone falls into

Fewer than one in five solo website builders earn most of their money from monthly plans, and two out of every three of them make under fifty thousand dollars a year. Those two facts are really the same fact wearing different clothes.

3. Add-on projects

Once a site is live, the owner will start asking you for things: online ordering, a booking calendar, a way to take payments, or a photo gallery for the second location.

Each of those is a separate job carrying a separate price, and this is where the real money hides, because those jobs are harder and far fewer people can do them. The industry data on this point is unusually clear. Artificial intelligence dropped the price of simple websites by sixty to eighty-five percent, while complicated work either held its price or went up.

Learn to do the complicated things, because that is the ground where you will not be undercut.

4. Referrals

The cheapest customer you will ever get is the one your last customer sends you, and this is not really a separate skill. It is simply what happens on its own when the first three lines are handled properly.

One restaurant owner tells another restaurant owner, and that is the entire marketing plan for your first year in business.

What you are not selling

Being clear about this will save you from taking jobs that eat you alive.

  • You are not a graphic designer, which means you do not spend forty hours drawing a logo by hand.
  • You are not a photographer, although you can generate images that look every bit as professional.
  • You are not a marketing agency running advertising campaigns with somebody else's money.
  • You are not a software company building custom tools from nothing.
  • You are not free technical support for every device the owner happens to own.

That last one matters far more than it sounds. Say yes to fixing the owner's printer just once, and you have quietly become the neighborhood computer guy, a job that pays badly and never ends.

The scarce resource here is not money at all. It is your attention held steady over several months, along with your willingness to be uncomfortable in front of strangers.

Who should not do this

An honest book has to tell you when walking away is the right move.

  • You cannot stand talking to strangers. The building is easy now, so the selling has become the actual job, and a conversation with a shop owner will fill some people with dread.
  • You need income this week. Your first real payment is realistically thirty to sixty days away, so if rent is due on Friday, please do not quit anything.
  • You want to be left alone to build. Monthly plans mean people call you, which is the good part financially and the annoying part personally.
  • You will not write things down. This business quietly collapses without checklists, and Part 3 will show you exactly why that happens.

None of those conditions are permanent. They are simply true today, and knowing which one applies to you is worth more than any technical lesson in this entire book.

Do this before the next module

Write your own sentence, keeping it to one sentence, describing what you sell and who you sell it to.

Then stand up and say it out loud to a wall, and if you stumble anywhere in the middle, then the sentence is too long or too vague and you should cut it down and try again.

Here are three examples that work:

  1. "I build and run websites for restaurants in south Seattle so they can take orders online."
  2. "I make sure barbershops show up whenever somebody nearby searches for a fade."
  3. "I keep small food businesses visible on the internet for a monthly fee."

Notice that none of them use the word website twice, and two of them do not lead with that word at all.

What you should have when you finish

A single spoken sentence describing your business, tested out loud against a wall.

A clear picture of the four income lines, along with an honest note about which one you have been ignoring.

A first-month budget under four hundred dollars, written down where you can see it.
