Why a checklist beats being careful

You will not remember everything, and neither will anybody else in this trade. That is not a character flaw at all, it is simply how attention works once you have looked at the same page forty times.

A written checklist takes the memory out of it entirely. You run the same twenty checks on every job in the same order, and the quality of your work stops depending on how tired you happened to be that day.

This module builds that checklist for you. Print it out, keep it beside you while you work, and never skip an item just because a site obviously passes.

Check one: the phone, properly

Shrinking a browser window is not a phone test at all, because a real phone has a different screen, a slower connection, and a thumb rather than a mouse pointer.

Open the site on an actual phone, using mobile data rather than wifi, and look for five specific failures:

  • Text that is small enough that you instinctively bring the phone closer to your own face.
  • Buttons sitting close enough together that your thumb regularly hits the wrong one of them.
  • Any need at all to scroll sideways, which should simply never happen on a well-built page.
  • Images that push the whole layout wider than the screen it is being viewed on.
  • A menu that requires two separate taps before anything readable appears on screen.

Nearly three-quarters of local searches happen on a phone, so a page that fails here is failing in the only place that matters.

Check two: speed, and the three fixes

Speed matters mainly because people leave, and on a slow connection they leave very quickly indeed.

You do not need deep technical knowledge to fix most of it, because three particular problems cause the great majority of slowness on small sites.

Oversized images. A photograph straight from a camera or a generator is often far larger than any screen actually needs, so resizing the large ones usually makes the page dramatically faster.

Too many separate images at the top. A gallery of twelve photographs near the top forces every visitor to download all of them before seeing anything at all, so move galleries lower down the page.

Video that plays automatically. It looks impressive on your own laptop and it quietly punishes anybody on a limited phone plan, so use a still image with a play button instead.

Test the site on a phone well away from your own wifi, count slowly to three, and see whether the main content has appeared yet. That crude little test catches nearly everything a beginner needs to catch.

Check three: the search basics

This is not the deep search work that agencies sell on a monthly basis, these are simply the four items that must be correct.

  1. The page title, which appears in the browser tab and in search results. It should read as business name, then what they do, then the city.
  2. The page description, one clear sentence that appears under the title in search results.
  3. The name, address, and phone number, written identically here and everywhere else the business appears online.
  4. The opening hours, correct and including holidays if the business closes for them.

That third item deserves real emphasis, because inconsistency quietly damages local search results. If the site says Avenue and the Google listing says Ave, then fix one of them so that they match exactly.

Check four: accessibility, which is now a legal matter

This section is not optional reading for anybody, and it matters especially if you plan to build for restaurants.

More than five thousand digital accessibility lawsuits were filed in 2025. Sixty-four percent of the businesses sued had under twenty-five million dollars in revenue, and roughly a fifth of the cases involved food service. These are small businesses, not corporations.

There is an important trap here. Businesses that installed accessibility widgets, meaning those small pop-up tools promising instant compliance, were sued at essentially the same rate as everyone else. The widget did not protect them, so do not sell one as a solution.

What actually helps is building the page properly in the first place, and five things cover most of what is needed:

  • Contrast. Use dark text on light backgrounds or light text on dark ones, because pale grey on white fails.
  • Alt text. Every meaningful image on the page needs a short written description of what it actually shows.
  • Headings in order. One main heading, then section headings underneath it, chosen for structure rather than for size.
  • Real buttons and links, so that both a keyboard and a screen reader are able to reach every one of them.
  • Text that can be enlarged by the reader without the whole layout collapsing in on itself.

You are not a lawyer and this book will never pretend you are. What you can honestly say to a client is that you build to the common standard from the start, which is more than most cheap builders do.

Check five: the things that embarrass you

These particular errors are small and stupid, and they are also the ones that clients actually notice.

  • The business name misspelled anywhere, including the browser tab.
  • A phone number the AI tool invented, which may belong to a real stranger.
  • An address that does not exist, or exists in the wrong city.
  • Placeholder prices left visible with no label.
  • A copyright year from a previous year at the bottom of the page.
  • Buttons that go nowhere when tapped.
  • Text still describing a different business, left over from an earlier draft.

Every one of these has happened to somebody before, usually in front of a paying client, and checking all of them takes about four minutes.

Do this before the next module

Run all twenty checks on the site you launched in Module 09, and write down every failure honestly.

You will fail some of them, and that is precisely the point of doing this on your own site first. Fix everything that failed, run the whole list again, and notice how much faster the second pass goes.

Then save the checklist somewhere that you will genuinely find it again, because Part 3 turns this single list into the beginning of a written operations manual.
