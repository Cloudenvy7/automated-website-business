Pricing, packaging and the $5,000 line

What to charge, why, and the single number that decides whether this business feeds you or drains you.

ManagerFog index 9.358 min read

Pricing is a decision, not a calculation

Most beginners try to work out their price by adding up hours, and that approach fails immediately in this business. If artificial intelligence builds the site in forty minutes, then hourly math says you should charge about sixty dollars for work that will earn the owner thousands.

Price is not a reward for your effort, it is a share of the value that the customer receives, and once you accept that sentence the rest of this module becomes straightforward.

Two owners can pay you very different amounts for an identical site and both prices can still be fair, because the restaurant gaining six extra orders a night got far more than the consultant who wanted a tidy business card online.

The number that should change your plan Across hundreds of small web shops surveyed in 2026, projects under five thousand dollars were profitable only about a third of the time. Projects above five thousand dollars were profitable more than half the time. The work is not twice as hard above that line, but the profit is.

Why cheap jobs lose money

This confuses people, so it is worth spelling out plainly.

A five-hundred-dollar job and a five-thousand-dollar job carry almost exactly the same fixed costs, because both need a first call, a quote, an intake, a review round, a launch check, and an invoice. That overhead does not shrink just because the price does.

What actually changes is the customer. Somebody paying five hundred dollars is usually buying on price alone, which means they compare you to a template, question every hour, and expect the same responsiveness as a larger client.

So the cheap job costs you nearly the same time, earns a tenth as much, and is more stressful throughout. That is the entire explanation for the profit cliff, and no amount of extra speed will ever fix it.

The three packages

Never present a single price, because a single price leaves the customer only two options, which are yes and no. Three prices quietly change the question from whether to buy into which one to buy.

Package one: Hand It Over

You build the site, connect it to a domain the client owns, hand over every file, and walk away. Future changes are separate work at a stated rate.

Price this between one thousand and two thousand dollars for a straightforward local business site. That sits inside the normal 2026 range, where most small shops charge one to three thousand dollars for a basic build.

Say the walk-away part out loud to them, because a client who believes they are buying an ongoing relationship will be quietly angry when the emails stop.

Package two: Keep It Running

You build the site and then you keep it working afterward. This is the package you actually want to sell, and the reason sits in the industry data rather than in your own preference.

Price it as a smaller build fee, often around half of package one, plus a monthly amount that never stops. The monthly figure is where beginners lose their nerve, so here is the honest range: across the industry, most shops charging for ongoing work land between five hundred and fifteen hundred dollars a month.

Package three: Build It Out

This is package two plus all the hard things: online ordering, booking, payments, delivery connections, and anything else that has to talk to another company's system.

Price the build somewhere between three and eight thousand dollars depending on what is being connected, and keep the monthly amount at the higher end. This package is where the profit cliff works in your favor rather than against you.

What is actually inside a care plan

Module 09 promised this section, because a monthly fee that nobody can describe is a monthly fee that gets cancelled in March.

Write down exactly what the client gets, and keep the list short enough to remember:

  • Changes. A named number of small updates each month, such as hours, prices, photos, and staff.
  • Watching. You check monthly that the site loads, the forms arrive, and nothing has broken quietly.
  • Renewals. You track the domain renewal date and tell them before it lands.
  • Backups. A saved copy of the site, so a bad edit is an inconvenience rather than a disaster.
  • Reachability. A stated response time, such as one business day, and a real way to reach you.
  • A quarterly note. Fifteen minutes of your time saying what changed and what you would do next.

Now write down what is not included, in that same document. New pages, new features, anything connecting to another company, photography, and rescuing a site somebody else broke are all separate jobs entirely.

Why the monthly number matters more than the build fee Shops with no recurring income lose money about fifty-eight percent of the time. Once a quarter of income arrives monthly, that figure drops below ten percent.

Yet fewer than one in five solo builders earn most of their money this way. That gap is the whole opportunity, and it is available to you on your very first client.

The mistake the last generation made

Back in 2016 roughly seventy percent of freelance designers already sold maintenance plans, so the idea itself is not new at all. What they charged was twenty-five to one hundred dollars a month.

At fifty dollars a month, twenty clients earns you one thousand dollars while creating twenty separate relationships that can each call you at any moment. That is not a business at all, it is a trap with a schedule attached.

The error was treating maintenance as a small favor attached to the real job. Price it as the real job, because for the client it genuinely is. Their site being visible, current and working every single day is worth more than the week you spent building it.

Choosing your actual numbers

Averages are useful for orientation and completely useless as a decision, so here is how to land on your own actual figures.

  1. Work out what you need monthly. Rent, food, car, phone, tools, and something for tax. Call that number N.
  2. Decide how many care clients you can genuinely hold. For one person doing this alongside another job, six to ten is realistic.
  3. Divide N by that number. That is your minimum monthly price. If it comes out under three hundred dollars, you are planning to be busy and poor.
  4. Set the build fee at three to five times the monthly. It covers the heavy first month and still makes the monthly look reasonable beside it.
  5. Write all three packages down on one page before you speak to anybody.

The written page matters considerably more than the numbers written on it, because a price you invented during a conversation always comes out lower than a price you decided the week before.

Saying the number out loud

This is the part that nobody ever practices and that almost everybody fumbles on the first attempt.

Say the price and then stop talking entirely. The silence afterward will feel far longer to you than it does to the customer, and beginners fill that silence by discounting themselves before anyone has actually objected.

Three sentences worth rehearsing until they sound ordinary:

  • "The build is fifteen hundred, and keeping it running is six hundred a month."
  • "That is the price for this package. If the budget is tighter, the hand-it-over package is a better fit."
  • "I can do it for less, but then it would be a different package, and I would rather show you what that looks like than quietly do less work."

Notice that the third one refuses a discount without refusing the customer.

Raising your price later

You will start out too low, because almost everybody does, and that is entirely repairable.

Raise the price for new customers only, and do it on the day a job first feels underpaid rather than at some future milestone. Existing clients keep their old rate until something changes for them, at which point the new rate applies.

There is one specific move here worth knowing about. When an existing client asks for something falling outside their plan, that request is the natural moment to quote current prices without any awkwardness at all.

Do this before the next module

Write your one-page price sheet with all three packages, real numbers, and the included and excluded lists for the care plan.

Then read the middle package out loud, exactly as you would to a customer, and listen for whether you flinch. If you do, the number is either wrong or you have not yet believed the value, and both problems get solved by writing down what the client gains rather than what you do.
