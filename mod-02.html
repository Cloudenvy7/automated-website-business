Part 1 — Ground floor · Module 02

Twenty years of this industry

What people sold in 2006, in 2016, and in 2026 — and what each change did to the person doing the selling.

MarketFog index 8.857 min read

Why history matters when you are broke

You might well be tempted to skip this module and get straight to the building, but please do not.

People have been selling websites to local businesses for more than twenty-five years. In that time, what they sold has changed completely three separate times, and each change made serious money for the people who saw it coming while quietly wiping out the people who did not.

You are arriving right at the start of the third change. If you know the pattern, you can aim at where the money is going, and if you do not know it, you will aim at where the money used to be and wonder why nobody wants to pay you.

2006: when knowing how was the whole job

Picture a small print shop in 2006, where the owner wants a website and has almost no way to get one himself.

Only forty-two percent of American adults had fast internet at home that year, and nearly a quarter of home users were still on dial-up as late as 2007. A website was a brochure that sat there quietly, not a store that took orders.

To build one, a person needed a real stack of separate skills:

  • Register a domain name, then point it at a server using settings almost nobody understood.
  • Rent hosting space and learn to move files onto it with a tool called FTP.
  • Design the page in Photoshop, then cut it apart and rebuild it in code by hand.
  • Test it in Internet Explorer, which held between eighty-two and eighty-six percent of the market and broke in its own special ways.
  • Write a small program just to make the contact form send an email.

The main professional tool, Dreamweaver 8, cost three hundred ninety-nine dollars. The United States government did not even have an official job code for "web developer" until 2010, which tells you how new and strange the work still was.

The point of 2006

The moat around this business was knowledge. A print shop owner could not do it himself, and he could not easily find somebody cheaper, because only a few people in his county could do it at all. That scarcity set the price.

2016: when templates took the bottom of the market

Ten years later the ground had shifted underneath everybody's feet, and it happened faster than most people noticed.

WordPress alone was running twenty-seven percent of every website on earth by late 2016, and Wix had ninety-seven million people signed up with two and a half million of them paying. Squarespace was buying commercials during the Super Bowl, where thirty seconds of airtime cost four and a half million dollars.

Suddenly the print shop owner had a real choice, because he could pay you, or he could pay twelve dollars a month and drag boxes around a screen himself.

Look closely at what that did to prices. In a survey of five thousand working web designers in 2016, ninety-five percent charged under five thousand dollars for a site, and only one percent ever cleared ten thousand dollars. Sixty-eight percent of them were working entirely alone.

Seventy percent of them were already selling a monthly maintenance plan, which sounds encouraging until you actually see the price, because those plans ran twenty-five to one hundred dollars a month.

What went wrong in 2016

Designers sold monthly plans at hobby prices. They treated maintenance as a small favor attached to the real job instead of treating it as the real job. That habit is still with us, and it is still the single most expensive mistake in this trade.

The interesting part is who saw it clearly. Wix said out loud in its 2016 financial filing that it competed with "template-based web builder companies and designers," and that it did not compete with traditional development firms.

Read that again, because the company was aiming directly at the freelancer's customer and it said so in public.

Meanwhile roughly half of American small businesses still had no website at all. That gap kept the low end of the market alive for a few more years, which is why a lot of people never noticed the floor giving way.

2026: when the building itself became nearly free

Now we arrive at today, which is a stranger moment than either of those two earlier years.

Artificial intelligence has cut the cost of a simple website by sixty to eighty-five percent, so a site that took real money three years ago now gets built in an afternoon. Nearly a quarter of shops using these tools quote under five hundred dollars, and about two-thirds of their projects finish inside two weeks.

At the same time, the easy first sale has mostly disappeared. Eighty-three percent of American small businesses already have a website, up from sixty-four percent in 2018.

So the two conditions that used to make this business work have both weakened at once, because building is no longer hard and almost everybody already has a site.

Here is where it gets interesting. The industry is still worth roughly forty-seven billion dollars a year in the United States, but employment in it has been falling about five percent a year.

That combination tells you something quite specific. The money did not leave the industry, it simply moved to a different place inside it.

Where the money actually went

Follow three particular numbers and the whole picture snaps into focus very quickly.

One. Shops with no monthly recurring income lose money about fifty-eight percent of the time. Shops where a quarter of income is recurring lose money less than ten percent of the time.

Two. Complicated work held its price or went up, even while simple work collapsed. The expensive part is now the thinking and the connecting, rather than the typing.

Three. Only about thirty-five percent of small businesses have a properly set up Google Business Profile, even though most customers find local businesses through search.

Put those together and you get the shape of the modern version of this job. You are not paid to make a website anymore. You are paid to make a local business findable, orderable, and reliable, month after month, using tools that happen to include a website.

The one-line version

2006: you got paid for knowing how. 2016: you got paid for doing it faster than the owner could. 2026: you get paid for what happens after launch.

The pattern underneath all three

Each era followed the same rule, and the rule is worth memorizing.

Whenever a task gets easy enough for anybody to do, the money stops living in that task and moves to the next hard thing beside it.

Hand-coding got easy, so money moved to design and templates. Templates got easy, so money moved to strategy and integration. Now building got easy, so money is moving to running, connecting, and being responsible.

This is not sad news at all, it is a map. It tells you which parts of the work to learn deeply, and which parts to hand to the machine without complaint.

Where this probably goes next

Nobody can promise you the future, so please treat this section as an educated guess rather than as a settled fact.

The signals worth watching are these. Freelance marketplaces are steadily losing buyers, while the money each remaining buyer spends keeps going up. On one major platform, work described as "AI integration" grew one hundred seventy-eight percent in a single year, while ordinary building work stayed flat.

That points toward a future where the person who wins locally is not the best builder. It is the person who is trusted with the whole system: the site, the ordering, the listing, the reviews, and the phone that gets answered.

Which is, when you think about it carefully, the same job the 2006 freelancer had. Only the hard part moved somewhere else.

Do this before the next module

Find one local business you already know. Look at their website on your phone, and then answer four questions in writing.

  1. Does it load quickly and read clearly on a phone screen?
  2. Can a customer order or book something without leaving the site?
  3. Does the business show up properly when you search its name plus your city?
  4. When was it obviously last updated?

You will usually find at least two clear problems, and those problems are your product. You have just done your first piece of real market research.

What you should have when you finish

The three-era story in your own words, short enough to tell a client in two minutes.

One sentence explaining why the build is cheap now but the care is not.

A written list of problems you found on one real local website.
