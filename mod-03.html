Background

The uncomfortable opening fact

Eighty-three percent of American small businesses already have a website, which is up from sixty-four percent in 2018.

Sit with that for a second, because it changes your whole approach. If you walk into a shop planning to say "you need a website," you will usually be talking to somebody who already has one and does not want a lecture about it.

The sale in 2026 is almost never a first website. It is a replacement, a repair, or an addition to something that already exists.

That is actually good news, and here is why. A person who already has a website has already accepted the idea that a website is worth money. You do not have to win that argument, you only have to win the comparison.

The three triggers that actually make somebody buy

Owners do not wake up wanting to spend money on the internet. Something specific has to push them, and in practice it is almost always one of three things.

Trigger one: it looks old

This is the most common and the easiest to spot. The photos are dark, the layout is cramped, the copyright line at the bottom says 2019.

The owner usually knows. They are a little embarrassed about it, and embarrassment is a powerful motivator once somebody offers a way out.

Fifty-eight percent of small businesses name a full redesign or visual refresh as their top planned investment for the coming year. That is the single biggest pile of ready money in this market.

Trigger two: it does not work on a phone

Pull the site up on your own phone in front of the owner and stay quiet while they look at it.

You will not need to say much. Text too small to read, buttons too small to hit, and a menu that requires pinching and dragging all make the argument for you.

Nearly three-quarters of local searches now happen on a phone, so a site that fails on a phone is failing at the only moment that counts.

Trigger three: it cannot take an order or a booking

This is the expensive trigger, and therefore the good one.

A restaurant with no online ordering is losing the customer who wanted food at 6:40 on Tuesday. A barbershop with no booking link is losing the customer who decided on a haircut at eleven at night.

About a quarter of all service appointments are now booked online. When a business cannot accept that, the loss is real money, and the owner can usually feel it without you explaining it.

How to open a conversation

Do not ask whether they like their website, because that invites a defensive answer.

Ask instead: "When somebody orders from you at night, what do they do?" Then listen carefully, because the gap in their answer is the job.

Who they hire, and where you fit

When small businesses do spend money on a website, here is who actually gets the work.

Look hard at that table, because it holds both bad news and good news for you.

The bad news is that the freelancer slice is small. Only about one job in eleven goes to somebody like you, and agencies take nearly half.

The good news is that agencies are expensive and slow by comparison. The typical agency engagement runs several thousand dollars a month, while do-it-yourself tools cost a few dozen dollars a month.

There is an enormous unserved space between those two prices, and almost nobody is standing in it. That gap is your entire business.

The gap nobody is filling

Here is the most useful number in this whole module. Only about thirty-five percent of small businesses have a properly established Google Business Profile.

Take a moment to understand what that means in practice. Roughly two out of three local businesses are effectively invisible in the exact place where customers look for them.

The supporting numbers make the case even sharper:

  • Eighty-four percent of consumers searched for a local business in the past three months.
  • Seventy-five percent decide which business to use in under thirty minutes.
  • Twenty-eight percent decide in under five minutes.

A complete profile makes customers far more likely to consider a business trustworthy, and it costs nothing but time to set up.

This is the single easiest way to prove your value to a stranger. You can fix it in an afternoon, the improvement is visible, and almost none of their competitors have bothered.

Why this matters for pricing

A website is something an owner can compare against a template and call expensive. Being found by people who are already searching is not comparable to anything, and it is worth real money in the register.

The risk hiding in restaurant websites

This part is not pleasant, but you need to know it, especially if you plan to serve food businesses.

More than five thousand digital accessibility lawsuits were filed in 2025. Sixty-four percent of the businesses sued had under twenty-five million dollars in yearly revenue, and about twenty-one percent of the cases involved food service.

These lawsuits generally claim that a website cannot be used by people with disabilities, particularly those using screen readers. The small business is usually not the villain here, it simply never knew.

There is a further wrinkle worth remembering. Businesses that installed accessibility widgets, the little pop-up tools that promise instant compliance, were sued at essentially the same rate. The widget did not protect them.

You are not a lawyer, and this book will never pretend otherwise. What you can do is build sites that meet the basic standards from the start, which is a real service and a real reason to be chosen over somebody cheaper.

Building your own customer picture

Industry averages will only take you so far, because your actual customers live within a few miles of you.

Write down answers to these five questions about the specific kind of business you want to serve.

  1. What trade are they in, and how many of them exist within a fifteen-minute drive of you?
  2. Roughly how much does one customer spend with them in a single visit?
  3. How does a new customer currently find them?
  4. Which of the three triggers is most likely to apply to them?
  5. What would one extra customer a day be worth to them in a month?

That fifth question is the one that sets your price. If one extra customer a day is worth three thousand dollars a month to a restaurant, then a fifteen-hundred-dollar site and a monthly plan stop sounding expensive.

Do this before the next module

Pick one trade, then walk or drive a single commercial street near you and list ten businesses in that trade.

For each one, check three things on your phone and mark them down:

  • Do they have a website at all, and does it work on a phone?
  • Can you order or book from it directly?
  • Does a proper Google listing appear when you search their name plus your city?

Ten businesses will take you about an hour. When you finish, you will have a list of real prospects with real, named problems, which is worth more than any amount of reading.

What you should have when you finish

A written description of one customer type, answering all five questions.

A list of ten real local businesses, each marked for website, ordering, and listing.

One sentence on what a single extra daily customer is worth to that kind of business.

Check yourself
