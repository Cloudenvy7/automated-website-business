Before you touch anything

This module assumes you have never built a website before, and that assumption is entirely deliberate, because everything here is written to be followed exactly the first time without any guessing.

You need three things in front of you before you start:

  • A paid account on an AI tool that can build sites, which normally costs about twenty dollars a month.
  • A real business to practice on, even an imaginary one, because vague practice teaches you almost nothing.
  • Forty-five uninterrupted minutes, which matters far more than most beginners expect it to.

We are going to build a site for a made-up business called Rosa's Tamales, a small family food business that sells at markets and wants to start taking orders. Use your own example instead if you already have one, but follow exactly the same order of steps.

One thing to decide first

Build this practice site on your own account, not on a client's account. You will make mistakes, and you want those mistakes to happen somewhere private where nobody is watching.

Step one: find the site builder

Open your AI tool and look for the section that creates websites, which is usually listed under a menu of extra tools rather than sitting on the main chat screen.

Once you are inside that section, the tool should already understand that you intend to build a site rather than have a conversation. You will often see a small label confirming this, and that label matters, because typing the same words into the ordinary chat window will get you a description of a website instead of an actual website.

If you cannot find it, type "create site" into the chat and see what appears. These menus move around every few months, so hunting for the right button is a normal part of the job rather than any sign that you did something wrong.

Step two: the prompt formula

This is the part that separates a usable first draft from a complete mess, and it is genuinely worth memorizing.

A good building prompt has six parts, always given in the same order:

  1. The business. What it is, where it is located, and what actually makes it different from the others.
  2. The pages. Name every one of them explicitly, because the tool will otherwise guess badly.
  3. The feeling. The mood you want a visitor to have within about three seconds of arriving.
  4. The colors and logo. Say them plainly and simply, even when you are not yet sure about them.
  5. The main image. Describe the scene exactly as you would describe it to a photographer.
  6. The stand-ins. Tell it to invent placeholder prices and items, so that nothing stalls the build.

That last part is the one beginners skip, and skipping it costs an hour. If you do not tell the tool to make up prices, it will either leave awkward gaps or stop and ask you questions you cannot answer yet.

The full prompt, written out

Build a one-page site for Rosa's Tamales, a family tamale business in Tacoma that sells at weekend markets and takes pickup orders.

Pages: a main page, an about us section, a menu section, and a contact section.

The feeling should be warm, family-run, and proud, not corporate or fancy.

Colors: deep red and warm cream, with a simple hand-drawn look. Logo is the word Rosa's in a friendly script with a small corn husk beside it.

Main image: a close-up of tamales being unwrapped on a wooden table in daylight, steam rising, hands visible.

The menu is not finalized yet, so invent five tamale items with realistic prices as stand-ins, and mark them clearly as placeholders.

Notice what that prompt carefully avoids doing. It uses no technical words at all, it never mentions code, and it does not apologize anywhere for being unsure.

Spelling genuinely does not matter here. These tools read intent, so a typo in your prompt will not change the result, and worrying about it just slows you down.

Step three: watch it build

Press enter and then leave the thing completely alone for roughly ten to fifteen minutes.

What happens during that time is that the tool writes the visible page, the underlying structure, and the images all at once. You will see it working through several stages, and the early stages nearly always look wrong or half-finished.

Do not interrupt it, and do not start typing corrections while it is still working, because interrupting a half-finished build is the most common way beginners waste twenty minutes.

Use the waiting time productively instead. This is a good moment to open a second window and begin the brand style guide covered in Module 07, since that work runs perfectly well alongside the build.

Step four: the four things it always gets wrong

Your first draft will be roughly eighty percent right, which feels like magic until you look at it closely, and the same four problems show up nearly every single time.

The menu is buried. The tool tends to put the food or service list near the bottom, below the story about the business. Real customers want the menu first, so move it up.

The prices look real. Placeholder prices are generated to look convincing, which is dangerous, because an owner may not realize they are invented. Label them clearly before anybody else sees the page.

The images are generic. The first pass often produces food that looks slightly plastic, or people who look like stock photos. Regenerate the important ones with a more specific description.

The contact details are wrong. The tool will confidently invent a phone number, an address, and hours. Every one of those must be replaced with real information before the site goes anywhere near a customer.

The invented phone number

Take this one seriously, because it is the mistake that will actually embarrass you. An AI tool will produce a plausible phone number without hesitating, and that number may belong to a real stranger. Check every digit.

Step five: fixing things by asking

You do not edit these sites the way you would edit a document in a word processor, you simply describe in plain language what you want changed.

Good correction requests are specific and name the thing being changed:

  • "Move the menu section so that it sits above the about us section on the page."
  • "Add the word PLACEHOLDER in small grey text beside every single price on the menu."
  • "Replace the main image with a wider shot showing a market stall and a line of waiting customers."
  • "Change every phone number to 253-555-0142 and the address to 1400 South Yakima Avenue."

Bad requests are vague, and vague requests waste turns:

  • "Make it better."
  • "I do not like the top part."
  • "Can you fix the colors?"

Change one thing at a time and look carefully at the result after each change, because stacking five requests into a single message usually gets three of them done and two silently ignored.

Step six: share it without giving away control

By default, only the account that created the site is able to see it at all, which is both correct and safe behavior.

To let somebody else look at it, open the site's share settings and change the visibility to anyone with the link, then copy that link and send it across.

Two things go wrong here often enough to warn you about them.

First, you may see an error mentioning an expired or missing token when previewing. This usually means the session has simply gone stale, so sign out, sign back in, and open the site again. It is not something that you broke.

Second, sending a preview link is not the same as delivering a website. The site still lives under your account, at an address that includes your account name. Making it live under the client's own name is a separate job, and Module 09 covers it properly.

Say this when you send a preview

"Here is a first draft. The prices and photos are placeholders, so ignore those. I want you to look at the layout and the feeling, and tell me what is wrong."

Naming the placeholders yourself keeps the conversation on the layout instead of the fake tamale prices.

What a finished first pass looks like

Before you show anybody, walk through this list and fix anything that fails.

  • The business name is spelled correctly in every place it appears, including the browser tab at the top.
  • The menu or service list appears before the long story about the history of the business.
  • Every invented price is clearly and visibly labeled as a placeholder that still needs replacing.
  • No invented phone number, address, or set of opening hours remains anywhere on the page.
  • The main image looks like an actual photograph rather than like a drawing or a cartoon.
  • The page loads properly on your phone, which you have checked yourself rather than assumed.
  • Every button on the page actually does something sensible when a person taps it.

That is seven checks and they take about five minutes in total. Doing them before a client sees the page is the whole difference between looking professional and looking careless.

Practice assignment

Build two sites this week, not one, and make them different on purpose.

The first should be the Rosa's Tamales example, followed exactly as it is written above, so that you learn the order of the steps.

The second should be a real business you know personally, built without looking back at this module. Struggling a little is the point, because it shows you which steps you actually absorbed.

Time yourself carefully on the second one. Most people land somewhere between forty and seventy minutes on a second attempt, and getting under thirty minutes usually takes about five builds.

What you should have when you finish

Two practice sites, one following the example and one from memory.

A shareable preview link for at least one of them, tested by opening it on your phone.

Your own copy of the seven-point check, kept somewhere you will actually see it.

A written time for your second build, so you can measure improvement later.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. List the six parts of a good building prompt, in order.

The business, the pages, the feeling, the colors and logo, the main image, and the stand-ins. The last one is the part beginners skip, and skipping it costs an hour.

2. Name the four things an AI build gets wrong nearly every time.

The menu is buried below the story, the placeholder prices look real, the images look generic, and the contact details are invented. That last one is the mistake that will actually embarrass you.

3. Why should you not narrate while a client looks at the draft?

Because they are trying to picture their own business in what they are seeing, and every sentence you say interrupts that picture. Watch their face instead — whatever is on screen when their expression changes is the thing they care about.
