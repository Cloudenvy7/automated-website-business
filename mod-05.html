What a website is made of

Domain, hosting, and DNS explained like plumbing, so you never have to bluff in front of a client again.

TechnicianFog index 9.046 min read

Why this module exists

You can build a working website today without understanding any of this, which is exactly the problem.

The trouble arrives later, usually in front of the client. They ask who owns the domain, or their email suddenly stops working, and you discover that you cannot answer because you never learned the parts. That moment costs you the relationship, and it is entirely avoidable with about twenty minutes of reading.

Every website is made of three separate things that people constantly confuse with each other. Learn to keep them apart and most of the confusion in this trade disappears.

The three parts, using a building

Picture a restaurant on a street corner, because the comparison holds up all the way through.

The domain is the street address. It is the name people type, like rosastamales.com, and you do not own it forever, you rent it yearly. Nobody else on earth can rent that exact name while you hold it.

The hosting is the building itself. It is the actual computer, sitting in a warehouse somewhere, that holds your pages and hands them to visitors. You rent this monthly or yearly, and if you stop paying, the building goes away.

The DNS is the sign pointing from the address to the building. When somebody types the domain, DNS is the system that says "the pages for that name live on this particular computer." It is a lookup table, nothing more.

The whole thing in one line A visitor types the domain, DNS tells their browser which host to ask, and the host hands back the pages. Three parts, one journey, every single time.

Most website problems you will ever encounter are one of these three parts failing, and being able to name which one is half the repair.

What changes when you use an AI site builder

Here is where modern tools help you and confuse you at the same time.

When you build a site with an AI tool, that tool handles the hosting for you automatically, so you never rent a building or move any files. It also gives you a free address, which usually looks something like yourname.chatgpt.site rather than a real business name.

That free address is fine for showing a draft, and it is completely wrong for a real business. It has somebody else's brand in it, it cannot be moved, and it quietly tells every customer that the business did not pay for its own name.

So the modern job is smaller than the old one, but the one remaining piece matters enormously. You are connecting a real domain that the client owns to a site that the tool hosts, and Module 09 walks through exactly how.

The question that decides everything

Whoever controls the domain controls the business online, and that sentence is worth reading twice.

If you register the domain under your own account and your own card, then you hold the keys. The client cannot move to another provider, cannot take the site elsewhere, and cannot even get their email working without you. Some people in this trade do that deliberately as a way of keeping clients.

Do not be one of those people, for two reasons that are both practical rather than moral.

The first reason is that clients eventually find out, and the story spreads through a small business community faster than any advertising you could buy. The second reason is that holding somebody's domain makes you responsible for it forever, including at two in the morning when it expires and you are asleep.

The rule, and it has no exceptionsThe client buys and owns the domain, using their own card and their own email address. You sit beside them while they do it, or you walk them through it on the phone. You may be given access, but you are never the owner.

The words clients will use

Owners rarely use the correct term, so part of your job is quiet translation. Here is what they usually mean.

  • "My website is down." Usually means it looks wrong, loads slowly, or they cannot find it in a search. Genuine outages are rare.
  • "Can you get me on Google?" Almost always means the Google Business Profile, the box with the map and the hours, rather than the website itself.
  • "I need my website updated." Could mean new hours, new photos, or a complete redesign. Always ask what changed in the business.
  • "The internet guy set it up." Means somebody years ago holds credentials nobody can find. Budget time for detective work.
  • "I have a website on Facebook." Means they have a Facebook page and no website. This is common and it is a real opportunity.

That last one deserves your attention, because a business living entirely on a social page is renting its whole presence from a company that can change the rules whenever it likes.

Finding out what a business already has

Before you can fix anything, you need to know what exists, and you can learn most of it in five minutes from your phone.

  1. Search the business name plus the city, then note whether a proper listing with a map appears.
  2. Open whatever website appears and check the address bar for the real domain name.
  3. Look at the bottom of the page for a copyright year, which usually reveals the last real update.
  4. Try the site on your phone and see whether anything is unreadable or unclickable.
  5. Ask the owner two questions: who set it up, and do you have the login.

That fifth question is the one that reveals the truth. A surprising number of small businesses cannot get into their own website, and rescuing that situation is a paid job all by itself.

Do this before the next module

Buy one domain name for yourself, even if you have nothing to put on it yet.

The point is not the name, it is the experience of doing it once with your own money before you ever guide a client through it. You will see the renewal options, the privacy settings, and the upsells they push at checkout, and none of that will surprise you later.

Then write down three things in a note: the registrar you used, the renewal date, and the price. That note is the first entry in the record-keeping habit that Part 3 turns into a system.
