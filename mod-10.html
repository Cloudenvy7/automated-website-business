Background

Why this is the most valuable module in the book

Everything up to now can be copied by anybody with an AI subscription and a free weekend, which is exactly why building sites no longer commands a real price.

This module is different. Connecting a business to ordering, booking and payments is the work that held its price while everything else collapsed, and the reason is simple. It touches somebody else's system, it can break, and somebody has to be responsible when it does.

There is a hierarchy here, and knowing where a job sits on it is most of the skill. Four levels, easiest first.

  1. A link. You point a button at a page somebody else hosts. Ten minutes.
  2. A snippet. You paste code the platform gives you into the site. Twenty minutes.
  3. A hosted page on their own domain. Ordering lives on their name, not the platform's. An afternoon, plus DNS.
  4. A real integration. Direct connection through a platform's developer programme. Weeks, agreements, and approval.

Most beginners assume everything is level four and never quote it. In reality most of what a small restaurant needs is level one or two, which you can genuinely deliver this week.

Two of the vendors below currently contradict themselves across their own pages, which tells you how carefully to tread.

The argument that sells the whole thing

Before any technical step, you need the sentence that makes an owner care. It comes from the commission difference, and it is large.

When an order arrives through a delivery marketplace, the restaurant loses a percentage of it. In August 2026 the published US rates look like this.

PlatformMarketplace deliveryPickupTheir own website
DoorDash15% Basic, 25% Plus, 30% Premier6%0% commission, ~2.9%–3.3% + $0.30 processing
Uber Eats20% Lite, 25% Plus, 30% Premium7%–10%2.5% + $0.29 processing
Grubhub5%/15%/20% marketing, plus 10%+ for deliveryvaries0% commission, processing fee not published

Now do the arithmetic in front of the owner, because it lands harder than any pitch.

A restaurant doing three hundred dollars a night through a marketplace at twenty-five percent is losing seventy-five dollars a night, which is roughly twenty-two hundred dollars a month. The same orders through their own site cost them the processing fee of about nine dollars a night instead.

That is the argument. You are not selling a button, you are selling the difference between those two numbers.

Be honest about the other side of it too. The marketplace brings them customers they would not otherwise have, so nobody should leave it entirely. The move is to shift the customers who already know the restaurant onto the direct channel, and keep the marketplace for discovery.

Level one: the order link

This is the fastest paid work available to you, and it takes about ten minutes.

If the restaurant is already on DoorDash, they already have a commission-free ordering page and most owners do not know it exists.

Step by step:

  1. Have the owner sign in to the DoorDash Merchant Portal, on their own account, with you beside them.
  2. Go to Channels, then Online Ordering.
  3. Copy the custom ordering link shown there.
  4. Open the site you built and add a button in the header saying Order Online.
  5. Point that button at the link, and set it to open in a new tab.
  6. Test it on a real phone, place a genuine order, and then cancel it if you can.

Grubhub has an equivalent for restaurants already on their marketplace, called Grubhub Direct, which imports the menu and photos automatically. Uber Eats has one called Webshop, set up in Uber Eats Manager, which generates links you can put on a site.

That is a real deliverable. The owner now takes orders on their own site at zero commission, and it cost you an afternoon at most.

Level two: the pasted snippet

A link sends people away, while a snippet keeps them on the page. It is a better experience and it prices higher.

DoorDash publishes a floating order button that you add by pasting a small piece of JavaScript into the site. You will need the business ID and a value called a slug from the merchant portal, and the button is tied to the Boost or Pro packages of their commerce platform rather than the free tier.

GloriaFood is the option worth knowing for a restaurant not on any marketplace. The core ordering system is free with no commission and no monthly fee, and the admin panel gives you an HTML snippet to paste straight into your page. Card payment through it is an add-on at about twenty-nine dollars a month.

Step by step for any snippet:

  1. Create the account on the client's email, never yours. This is the Module 05 rule and it applies here exactly.
  2. Build the menu inside that platform, or import it if the platform offers that.
  3. Find the publishing or website section and copy the snippet.
  4. Paste it into the site where the platform's instructions say, which is usually just before the closing body tag or into a custom code block.
  5. Reload the page and confirm the button appears.
  6. Place a real order end to end. Do not skip this.
  7. Confirm the order actually reached the restaurant, on their tablet or in their email.

Step seven is the one beginners skip and it is the one that matters. A button that looks right and delivers nothing is worse than no button at all.

The test order ruleNever hand over an ordering system you have not ordered through yourself, with real money, all the way to the restaurant confirming they saw it.

Then place a second one during their actual service hours, because plenty of systems behave differently when the kitchen is busy.

Level two, other flavors: booking and payments

The same pattern covers appointments and money, and both are simpler than ordering.

Booking. Setmore has a free tier including a website booking widget, capped at two hundred appointments a month, which suits a small barber or a single practitioner. Calendly's free plan allows one event type, and removing their branding needs a paid plan at around ten dollars a seat. Square Appointments is free with unlimited staff calendars if the business already uses Square, with paid tiers at forty-nine and one hundred forty-nine dollars a month per location. Acuity has no free plan and starts around twenty dollars a month.

All of them work the same way: create the account on the client's email, set up the services and hours, copy the embed code, paste it into a page on the site, and test a real booking through to their calendar.

Payments. Stripe Payment Links are the beginner's friend here, because they need no code at all. You create a link for a product or a deposit, and you can share it as a URL or add it to the site as a buy button. Stripe's standard US rate in August 2026 is 2.9% plus thirty cents for domestic cards, with no monthly fee. Square and PayPal are comparable, with PayPal's checkout at 3.49% plus a fixed fee and its card processing at 2.99%.

Level three: their own domain

This is where you start earning properly, and it is mostly domain work rather than payment work.

The pattern is that an ordering system lives on the platform's address by default, so a customer clicking Order Online lands somewhere that does not look like the restaurant. Moving it onto the client's own name makes the whole thing feel like one business.

Square documents this route plainly. You create the ordering page, publish it to a domain, and then link to it from the main site, using either a free subdomain, a custom subdomain, or a domain the client already owns. Toast's higher ordering tier is hosted directly on the client's own domain.

The work is the same DNS work from Module 09, applied to a subdomain such as order.theirbusiness.com. Nothing new to learn, and it is worth real money because it looks like a professional job rather than a bolted-on link.

Level four: the real integrations, and why you say no

Now the honest part, which protects you more than any skill in this module.

A direct connection to a delivery platform's ordering system is not a bigger version of the work above, it is a different category with gatekeepers.

Uber Eats requires a developer account, a signed non-disclosure agreement and an API licensing agreement, and states that access may require written approval. Their own documentation gives a timeline of four to eight weeks from test setup to launching stores, and a partner manager has to confirm the integration before development starts.

DoorDash requires passing a certification process before production access is granted. Partners commit to a fourteen-day onboarding timeline, a forty-eight business-hour window to resolve menu issues, and must keep order failure and merchant cancellation rates below one percent.

Read those requirements as what they are. They are written for point-of-sale companies and platform vendors serving many restaurants, not for one person building for one client. Nothing forbids you from applying, and everything about the structure assumes an ongoing operator with staff.

There is a second category you should also refuse. Building a card entry form on the client's own site moves them from the lightest level of payment security paperwork to a heavier one with an annual attestation. Hosted checkout, where the card fields live on the payment company's page inside a frame, keeps them on the light version. Always take the hosted route.

What to say when asked for level four"That specific connection goes through their developer programme, which needs an agreement with them and runs four to eight weeks minimum. I can do it with a specialist I work with, and it is a separate project at a separate price.

What I would do first is the version that takes orders on your own site this week at zero commission, and then we look at whether the deeper one is worth it."

You have not said no. You have offered them something better this week, which is usually what they actually wanted.

Two rules keep you out of trouble. Never quote an integration you have not done before at a fixed price, because your first one always takes three times as long as your third. And never absorb the platform's fees into your price, since those are the client's ongoing costs and they must see them clearly.

Before you quote anything, check these five

Ten minutes of checking prevents most disasters.

  1. Which platforms are they already on? An existing DoorDash account makes the fastest job available immediately.
  2. Who has the logins? If nobody can get into the merchant portal, that recovery is its own job.
  3. Where do orders land? Tablet, printer, email, or point-of-sale, and does the owner actually watch it.
  4. Do they already have a card processor? Adding a second one creates reconciliation problems for them.
  5. What are they paying in commission today? This is your sales argument and it takes one question.

Do this before you finish this part

Pick a real local restaurant, ideally one already on a delivery platform, and do the level one job for free as practice.

Walk them through finding their own ordering link, add the button to a site you build, and place a real order through it. You will learn more from that one afternoon than from re-reading this page.

Then write down, in your own words, the sentence you would use to explain the commission difference to an owner. That sentence is the most valuable thing in this module, and it should take you about fifteen seconds to say.
