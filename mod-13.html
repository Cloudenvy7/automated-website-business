The seven-step delivery pipeline

From first hello to handoff, run the same way every time, so the quality never depends on your mood.

ManagerFog index 9.357 min read

Why the same order every time

A first job feels like an adventure and a fifth job feels like a mess, and the whole reason for that difference is that nothing ever got written down in between them.

Without a fixed order you will forget the deposit on one job, skip the phone check on another, and let a third drift for six weeks because nobody agreed what happens next. None of those are skill problems, they are ordering problems.

A pipeline fixes this by deciding once, in advance, what happens and in what order it happens. After that, every job becomes the same job with different words filled into it.

The seven steps

Here is the whole thing on a single page. Each step carries a condition that must be true before you move on, and that condition is the part which actually keeps a job from stalling.

#StepMove on when
1First callYou know the business, the trigger, and the budget range
2QuoteThey picked a package in writing
3Deposit and intakeMoney received and the intake form is back
4BuildThe draft passes your own seven-point check
5ReviewThey have given you one consolidated list of changes
6LaunchThe twenty-point check passes on a real phone
7Handoff or start the planThey have the files, or the first monthly payment is set up

Print that table out, because everything below it is simply the detail sitting behind each row.

Step 1: the first call

Give this fifteen to twenty-five minutes, and understand that you are not selling yet, you are finding out whether there is a real job here at all.

Three things must come out of this call: what the business actually does, which of the three buying triggers is pushing them right now, and roughly what they are willing to spend.

That third one feels rude to ask and it is genuinely the kindest question in the whole process, because a ten-minute conversation ending in "my budget is two hundred dollars" saves you both a week of polite disappointment.

Ask it plainly: "Most of what I do lands between one and three thousand to build, plus a monthly amount. Does that sound like the right neighborhood for you?"

End every first call the same way "I am going to write this up and send you three options tomorrow morning. Have a look, tell me which one fits, and then we will talk about dates."

You have now set a deadline for yourself, told them exactly what to expect, and made the next move obvious.

Step 2: the quote

Send the quote the next day rather than the next week, because interest cools far faster than most beginners expect it to.

Keep it to one page containing five things: what you understood about their business, the three packages with real prices, what they get in each, when it would be finished, and what happens next.

Write the first line in their words rather than yours. When the quote opens with "you told me people call after nine at night and there is no way for them to order," the owner knows they were listened to.

Then stop and leave it alone. Do not follow up the same day, do not add a discount nobody asked for, and do not send a second version simply because you thought of something better.

Step 3: deposit and intake

Nothing gets built before money moves, and this is not really about distrust at all. It is about attention, because a client who has paid something actually turns up for the review call.

Take half of the build fee up front, and if the whole build comes in under a thousand dollars, then simply take all of it.

At that same moment, send the intake form across, and keep it short, because a long form simply does not get returned:

  • Business name exactly as it should appear, including any punctuation
  • Phone, address, hours, including holiday closures
  • The menu or service list with prices, or a note saying it is coming
  • Any logo, photos or colors they already have
  • Their three favorite competitor sites, and one sentence on why

That last question is the one that saves you a round of revisions, because it tells you their taste without either of you needing design vocabulary.

Step 4: build

This step is Modules 06 through 09 in practice, and the pipeline has only one extra rule to add to them.

Do not show anybody anything until it passes your own seven-point check. Showing a rough draft feels collaborative and it actually costs you money, because the client starts commenting on the wrong things and you spend the review discussing a placeholder phone number.

If something is still missing, then use a clearly labeled placeholder and keep moving forward rather than waiting around.

Step 5: review

Send the preview along with three specific instructions, because an open-ended request produces feedback that trickles in for a fortnight.

Tell them plainly: look at it on your phone, send me everything in one single message, and I need it by Thursday.

Then hold the line on one round. One consolidated round of changes is included, a second round is fine if it is small, and a third round means the job has changed and needs a new quote. Module 16 covers how to say that without a fight.

The stall that kills small jobs The most common way a website job dies is not rejection, it is silence. The client goes quiet at step 3 or step 5, usually because they owe you something and feel bad about it.

Break it with a message that is easy to answer: "No rush at all. Is the menu still coming, or would you rather I put stand-in prices up and we fix them after launch?" You have given them a way out that keeps the job alive.

Step 6: launch

Run the twenty-point check from Module 11 in full, including all the parts that you are already sure will pass.

Then send the launch message from Module 09, naming the new address, everything you tested, and the renewal date.

Launch day is also invoice day for the remaining balance. Sending it the same afternoon feels pushy for about ten seconds and then feels completely normal, whereas sending it two weeks later feels like an interruption.

Step 7: handoff or start the plan

This final step exists because leaving it out is exactly how people end up doing free work for years afterward.

If they bought the hand-it-over package, deliver the client folder, confirm they can sign into everything, and say out loud that future changes are separate work at a stated rate. Then actually stop.

If they bought a care plan, this is the day the monthly payment starts. Set it up while you are talking to them, because a plan that starts "next month" often never starts at all.

Either way, put one thing in your calendar before you close the laptop: a check-in thirty days out. That single reminder is the difference between one job and a client.

How long the whole thing should take

Expect roughly two to four weeks from the first call through to launch, which matches what most small shops report for a straightforward site.

Almost none of that time is actual building. The build itself is a day or two and the remainder is waiting on people, which is exactly why the pipeline sets conditions rather than dates.

If a job passes six weeks, something is stuck at step 3 or step 5 and it will not unstick itself.

Do this before the next module

Take the last thing you built, even a practice site, and write it up as though it had gone through all seven steps.

Write the actual words for each one: the questions you would ask on the call, the quote, the intake list, the review instructions, and the launch message. You are not inventing a process here, you are writing down the one you just used, which is far easier.

Keep all of it in a single folder, because that folder is the beginning of your operations manual, which Module 12 turns into a real system.
