Words that sell the business

The five blocks of writing every local site needs, and how to get them out of an owner in a single phone call.

TechnicianFog index 8.537 min read

Why the writing is the hard part now

The tools now handle layout, color, and images without much help from you at all, which means the words have become the last part of this job that still requires an actual human being.

They are also the part that clients notice most, because an owner will glance at the design and then read every single word written about their own business. Those words are the story they have been telling in person for years.

Get the words right and small design flaws stop mattering to anybody, while getting them wrong means that no amount of visual polish will save the page.

The five blocks

Nearly every successful local business site is built from the same five blocks, arranged in the same order.

One: the headline. A single line at the very top saying what this business is and where it is located. It is not a slogan and not a welcome message, just plain information stated clearly.

Two: the offer. The menu, the service list, or the price list, which belongs high on the page because it is the thing visitors actually came for.

Three: the story. Who runs this place, how long it has been going, and why it exists at all, told in a way that is short, warm, and specific.

Four: the proof. Reviews, years in business, photographs of real people, or an award, meaning anything that shows other humans have already trusted this place.

Five: the way in. The phone number, address, hours, and a button that starts an order or a booking, all of which must also appear at the very bottom, because that is where people look for it.

The ordering mistake to avoid AI tools love putting the story first, because a business description makes a natural opening paragraph. Real customers want the menu first. Move the offer above the story on nearly every site you build.

Getting the words out of the owner

You cannot write about a business that you do not understand, and owners are usually quite bad at describing themselves in writing. What they are good at is talking, so your job is to ask questions and take careful notes.

Six questions will get you everything you need in about fifteen minutes of conversation.

  1. "If somebody has never been here, what should they order the first time, and why that?"
  2. "Who comes in most often, and what are they usually doing that day?"
  3. "What do people say about you when they leave?"
  4. "What do you do differently from the place down the street?"
  5. "How did this get started?"
  6. "What do you wish more people knew about you?"

Write down their exact phrases rather than summarizing them into your own words. When an owner says "we still hand-roll every one at five in the morning," that sentence is worth more than any paragraph you could compose, and it belongs on the site word for word.

Question three is quietly the most valuable one of the six, because whatever customers already say is the strongest headline you will ever have. It is proven language rather than invented language.

Writing in their voice instead of yours

Once you have the notes in hand, the temptation is to smooth everything into polite business English, and you should resist that, because polite business English is exactly what every competitor already sounds like.

Three habits keep the owner's voice intact:

  • Keep their nouns. If the owner says tamales, then you do not write "authentic handcrafted cuisine" instead.
  • Keep their numbers. Twenty-two years, five in the morning, three generations, because specific numbers are believable in a way that adjectives never are.
  • Cut every word that could describe any business at all. Quality, passion, dedication, excellence, and commitment can all be deleted from any page with no loss whatsoever.

Here is the very same business written both ways, so that you can feel the difference for yourself.

Two versions of the same about sectionGeneric: "Rosa's Tamales is committed to providing quality food with a passion for authentic flavors and excellent customer service."

Theirs: "Rosa started selling tamales out of a cooler at the Tacoma market in 2004. We still hand-roll every one, starting at five in the morning, and we still sell out most Saturdays by noon."

The second version took no more skill to write than the first one did, it simply used the notes instead of ignoring them.

Handling prices on the page

Owners ask about this constantly, and the honest answer depends entirely on what it is that they sell.

Show prices when the item is simple and the number is normal for that category, which covers food, haircuts, and oil changes. Hiding the price of a burrito only makes people suspicious.

Do not show prices when the job varies enormously depending on the situation, such as roofing, catering, or legal work, because publishing a number there either scares people off or traps you into it.

Show a starting price when you want to filter out people who cannot afford the service, which is a legitimate goal for higher-end work.

There is a further wrinkle that applies to restaurants specifically. Menu prices change far more often than owners expect, so agree in advance on who updates them and how quickly, because that single conversation prevents most maintenance arguments later.

The stand-in trick

A job stalls when the owner has not sent you something they promised, and the missing thing is almost always the menu or the price list.

Do not sit around waiting for it. Fill the page with clearly labeled placeholders and keep the job moving forward.

Write the placeholder text so that it is impossible to mistake for real content, using the word PLACEHOLDER in capital letters and in a visible color beside every invented item. Then send the draft across anyway.

This works because looking at a wrong menu makes an owner fix it immediately, while looking at an empty page produces nothing at all. People correct far more readily than they create, and you can use that fact to keep a job moving.

Your reusable copy sheet

Do not start from an empty page on the next job you take. Build a fill-in-the-blank sheet once and then reuse it forever afterward.

That is five rows, and with it every future job becomes a matter of filling in a form rather than facing an empty screen.

Do this before the next module

Call or visit one real local business owner and ask the six questions, taking notes on their exact words.

You are not selling anything at all on this visit, and you should say so plainly at the very start, because it removes the pressure from both sides. Tell them you are learning to build websites and that you want fifteen minutes of practice.

Then write the five blocks straight from your notes, and read the story section out loud to yourself. If it sounds like a brochure, then you smoothed away the owner's voice somewhere and you should go back to the notes.
