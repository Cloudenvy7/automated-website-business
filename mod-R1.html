How to use this

Two columns in your head for every word here. What it means, and what it means to you when a client says it.

The second one is usually the useful part, because clients rarely use these words correctly and your job is quiet translation.

The website words

Domain. The address people type, such as rosastamales.com. Rented yearly, usually twelve to twenty dollars. To you: the client buys it, in their name, always.

Registrar. The company you rent the domain from. To you: write down which one, because a year from now nobody will remember.

Hosting. The computer that stores the pages and hands them to visitors. To you: usually included free when you build with an AI site tool, so you rarely buy this separately.

DNS. The lookup system that connects a domain to the hosting. To you: two or three settings you copy from one screen to another, and the most common place a launch goes wrong.

Subdomain. A name in front of the domain, such as order.rosastamales.com. To you: how you put an ordering system on the client's own name.

SSL, or the padlock. The thing that makes an address start with https and shows a padlock. To you: check it exists before you say a site is live.

Front end. What the visitor sees. Back end. The machinery behind it. To you: AI tools build both at once, which is why this distinction matters less than it used to.

CMS. Content management system, meaning software that lets somebody update a site without code. WordPress is the best known. To you: worth knowing the word, since many existing client sites are built on one.

Plugin. An add-on that gives a site an extra ability. To you: mostly a WordPress word, and mostly somebody else's problem.

Integration. Connecting a site to another company's system, such as ordering or booking. To you: the expensive work, covered in Module 10.

Responsive. A site that works on a phone as well as a laptop. To you: not a feature you sell, it is a minimum you check.

Alt text. A short written description of an image, for people using screen readers. To you: part of the accessibility work in Module 11, and a real legal consideration.

Google Business Profile. The box with the map, hours and reviews that appears when somebody searches a local business. To you: free, fast, skipped by roughly two-thirds of businesses, and the quickest way to prove your value.

The money words

Build fee. The one-time payment for making the site. To you: the smallest and least reliable of your four income lines.

Care plan, or retainer. A monthly payment for keeping the site working. To you: the number that decides whether this business survives.

Recurring revenue. Income that arrives on a schedule without a new sale. To you: the single most important idea in this whole book.

Deposit. Money taken before work starts, usually half. To you: not distrust, attention. Clients who have paid something turn up.

Due on receipt. Payment expected when the invoice arrives, rather than in thirty days. To you: the correct terms for small local clients.

Net 30. Payment due within thirty days. To you: a convention from large companies. Do not copy it.

Change order. A short written note saying what was newly requested, what it costs, and what it does to the timeline. To you: three sentences that protect an entire job.

Scope. What was agreed to be included. To you: write it down before you build, or you will argue about memory later.

Processing fee. What a payment company takes per transaction, commonly around 2.9% plus thirty cents. To you: the client's ongoing cost, and it must be visible to them, never buried in your price.

Commission. What a delivery marketplace takes from an order, commonly 15% to 30%. To you: the comparison that sells ordering work.

The business words

Sole proprietor. The default: you and the business are legally the same. Free and automatic. To you: fine while learning.

LLC. A registered company that separates business liability from your personal property. Fifty to five hundred dollars to start, depending on the state. To you: worth doing once real money moves.

The three roles. Technician does the work, Manager organizes it, Entrepreneur decides where it goes. To you: knowing which hat you are wearing today.

Working on it, not in it. Building the system that does the work, rather than only doing the work. To you: the line the whole book hangs from.

Operations manual. The written version of how you do everything. To you: Module 12, and the difference between a business and a job.

Lane, or niche. The one trade you serve first. To you: narrowing does not shrink your market, it shortens the distance to the people already looking.

Demographics. Who the customer is. Psychographics. Why they buy. To you: the second one writes your pitch.

Business Model Canvas. Nine boxes describing how a business works, on one page. To you: Module 20, and a twenty-minute exercise rather than a business plan.

Validated learning. Proof that cost somebody something, as opposed to a compliment. To you: did it cost them anything? If not, it is not evidence.

Position agreement. A written statement of the result somebody is accountable for, signed by both people. To you: what you write instead of a job description when you eventually hand work off.

The words clients use, translated

"My website is down." Usually means it looks wrong or they cannot find it in search. Genuine outages are rare.

"Can you get me on Google?" Almost always means the Google Business Profile, not the website.

"I need it updated." Could be new hours or a full redesign. Ask what changed in the business.

"The internet guy set it up." Somebody years ago holds credentials nobody can find. Budget time for detective work.

"I have a website on Facebook." They have a social page and no website. Common, and a real opportunity.

"Can you just quickly..." The start of a change order. See Module 16.
