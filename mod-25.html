Background

The question that stops nearly every project

I sat down with a client last week who wanted a Google site for her business. We spent the first hour on the enjoyable part, which was wiring her books through to a simple dashboard. Then she asked the question that stops nearly every project dead in its tracks.

"How do I decide what actually goes on it?"

She was not asking me about design at all. She was asking about her own Google Drive, which by that point held nine years of files. There were menus, price lists, photos, a logo saved in four slightly different versions, three competing drafts of an About page, and a folder named "New Folder (2)".

She could not choose what to publish because she genuinely did not know what she had.

Then she asked the second question, which is the better of the two. "Could that part be automated?"

This module answers her first question, and the module after it answers her second one. Between them they cover what is probably the most valuable hour of work you will ever sell to a small business.

The problem is not laziness

It is tempting to decide that a client in this state is simply disorganized. That reading is wrong, and holding it will make you impatient with somebody who deserves the exact opposite from you.

Researchers have studied this pattern for years and given it a name, which is digital hoarding. Neave and colleagues published the first proper measure of it in 2019, working first with 424 people and then with a second group of 203. A follow-up study in 2020 sorted the behavior into four separate drivers. All four are worth knowing, because you will meet every one of them in the field.

Anxiety keeps the file in case somebody needs it later. Disengagement makes sorting feel like a chore that pays no visible reward. Compliance means somebody once told them to keep records, so now they keep all of them forever. Collection makes the files feel like proof of the work, so throwing one away feels a little like erasing history.

None of that is laziness, and all of it is a fairly rational response to a folder that grew one file at a time across nine years.

There is a second finding that matters even more for how you sell this. A 2022 study by Nissim and colleagues, working with 465 people, found that the strongest single predictor of whether somebody organizes their files is self-efficacy. That is a formal way of saying that people sort their files when they believe they are capable of sorting them.

Your client does not believe she is capable, and that is precisely why she is paying you.

What this means for the sales conversation

Never tell a client to "get their content together" before you start. That sentence hands the single hardest task in the project to the one person least equipped to do it, and the project usually dies right there.

You do the first pass and they approve it. That order is the entire trick.

An inventory and an audit are two different jobs

These two words get used as though they mean the same thing, and they do not. Keeping them apart in your head will save you a surprising number of hours.

An inventory is a count: what is there, where it lives, and when somebody last touched it. Building one is honest clerical work that requires neither taste nor judgment, which is exactly why you can do it alone.

An audit is a verdict: whether the thing is any good, whether it is still true, and whether it belongs on the site. That requires judgment, and much of the time it also requires the client sitting next to you.

The distinction is not perfectly settled in the field, and Erin Kissane muddies it deliberately by calling an inventory a "quantitative audit". Use whichever words your client already uses, but keep the two jobs apart in your own mind, because one of them you can finish alone and the other one you cannot.

The practice is older than most people assume. Jeffrey Veen published the original working method in June 2002. His columns are still remarkably close to what people use today: an identifier, a name, a link, a document type, keywords, an owner, a status, and a notes field.

A research trap worth flagging now

Veen's original article lives on a website that has since been taken over, and it now quietly serves gambling spam. Do not send a link to a client, and do not send your own team there either.

The method survived perfectly well. The website did not.

The eight columns

Every serious version of this list turns out to be a variation on the same eight things. The Government of British Columbia publishes one of the fullest versions. Both the United States 18F team and the Nielsen Norman Group publish close cousins of it.

Here is the working set you should actually use.

ColumnWhat goes in it
IDA number you assign, so two files both called "menu" can be told apart
NameThe file name exactly as it stands today, mistakes included
LinkWhere the thing actually lives right now
TypePhoto, menu, price list, policy, logo, or draft
TopicOne or two words drawn from a fixed list that you control
OwnerThe one person who can answer questions about this item
StatusKeep, fix, or retire
NotesAnything a human being needs to know before touching it

That table is the entire information system, and it fits comfortably on a single screen.

The Status column is doing most of the heavy lifting there, and it comes from an older idea with a memorable name.

ROT

Redundant, Outdated, Trivial: three separate reasons a file should not go anywhere near the finished website.

Redundant means you already have the same thing somewhere else in the pile. Outdated means it was perfectly true once and is not true now. Trivial means nobody has ever needed it and nobody is realistically going to.

Mark every file with one of those three words or with "keep", and remember that you are not deleting a single thing at this stage. That distinction matters enormously to a client carrying the anxiety driver, so say it out loud on the call rather than assuming they know.

What the evidence actually says

Here I have to be straight with you, because you are going to be tempted to oversell this part to a client who is already nodding along.

There is no published study showing that cataloguing first prevents projects from stalling. I went looking for one, and if somebody tells you it exists, ask them politely for the paper.

What does exist is solid evidence about how much of a typical pile turns out to be dead weight. The UK government audited 723 pages in 2016 and found that 4.6% needed updating while 10.9% needed retiring altogether. When they narrowed the sample to guidance pages the numbers climbed to 7.2% and 17.8%. Their transport department worked through 7,396 separate items over six months.

So the honest sentence to say to a client is roughly this. About one item in six is going to turn out to be wrong or worth retiring, and there is no way to tell which sixth until somebody actually looks.

That is a claim you can defend if it is challenged. "Projects fail without this step" is not.

Grouping, and the number that is not real

Once every file carries a topic word, you group the topics themselves, and those groups are what become the pages on the finished site.

The formal name for a fixed list of topic words is a controlled vocabulary, and there is an actual standard covering it called ANSI/NISO Z39.19. The underlying idea is simply that everybody uses the same word for the same thing. If half the files say "menu" and the other half say "food list", then neither searching nor sorting is going to work properly.

The opposite approach is called a folksonomy, a word Thomas Vander Wal coined in July 2004. It describes what happens when everybody tags freely using whatever words come to mind. That is precisely what your client's Drive already is.

Now for the number. Sooner or later somebody will tell you that a menu should hold seven items, plus or minus two. Nearly everybody attributes that rule to a paper by George Miller.

Miller was writing about how many things a person can hold in memory at once, and he said in the paper itself that the number seven was "only a coincidence". A menu sits on the screen in front of the reader, who is therefore not recalling anything at all. Later work by Nelson Cowan in 2001 revised the memory figure down to about four in any case.

No authority anywhere prescribes a number of top-level pages. Anybody who quotes you one is repeating a myth they inherited. Group the files by what they honestly are, and stop splitting when the groups stop being obvious to a stranger.

If you want the client's view of the grouping rather than your own, the method for that is card sorting. Jakob Nielsen recommends fifteen participants, which gets you a 0.90 correlation with the true result. The method degrades once you push past roughly fifty cards, so sort topics rather than individual files.

Naming, where the experts disagree with each other

You will want to leave the client with a naming rule, so that files arriving next month show up already tidy instead of joining the pile.

The points that every authority agrees on are these. Dates go in year-month-day order so that files sort correctly on their own. Spaces are out, numbers carry leading zeros so that 02 lands before 10, and names stay short.

The points they emphatically do not agree on are the separator and the length. The United States National Archives says hyphens and underscores are both acceptable and suggests 25 to 35 characters. The University of Edinburgh says use capital letters instead of spaces or underscores. Harvard Medical School allows 40 to 50 characters, while the Smithsonian caps it at 25.

Pick whichever one you like and write it into the client's manual, because the rule being consistent matters far more than the rule being correct.

Why this is the start of a document management system

What you have built at this point is an index. In the trade a document management system means rather more than that: the file itself, the information describing it, and the rules that keep both of those honest over time.

The records standard ISO 15489 lists four things an authoritative record needs. It has to be authentic, meaning it is what it claims to be. Reliable, meaning its contents can be trusted. Whole, meaning nothing has been altered without leaving a trace. And useable, meaning somebody can still find it and open it years later.

Your spreadsheet delivers that fourth one on day one, and it is the honest beginning of the other three, which is worth something and worth selling.

But be careful with how you describe it.

Say this to the client before they say it to you

A spreadsheet catalogue is a photograph, not a mirror. The moment somebody renames a file, moves it into another folder, or deletes it outright, your catalogue is wrong and absolutely nothing warns you about it.

That is fine, and it is fixable, and it is the subject of the next module. What is not fine is selling a static list as a document management system and letting the client discover the gap in month three.

What you actually do on Monday

Three hours of work, split deliberately across two separate sessions.

  1. Alone, ninety minutes. Build the raw list: every file, all eight columns, topic words invented as you go. Do not pass judgment on anything yet.
  2. Alone, thirty minutes. Collapse your invented topic words down into one fixed set, merging "food list" into "menu", and then sort the whole table by topic.
  3. With the client, sixty minutes. Walk the table together while they call each row keep, fix, or retire, and you type. They will move faster than you expect, because deciding about one row is far easier than deciding about a folder.

By the end of that third hour the client can answer the question she opened with, and that answer is the material the site actually gets built out of.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. A client says she cannot decide what goes on her site. What is actually stopping her?

She does not know what she has. Nine years of files means she cannot answer a question about her own material, so every choice feels like a guess. That is a cataloguing problem wearing the costume of a design problem.

2. What is the difference between an inventory and an audit, and why does it change how you work?

An inventory is a count of what exists, which takes no judgment, so you can do it alone. An audit is a verdict on whether each thing is good, true and worth publishing, which usually needs the client sitting beside you.

3. Somebody tells you a site menu should hold seven items, plus or minus two. What do you say?

That rule is a myth built on a misread of Miller's paper, which was about memory and said the number seven was only a coincidence. A menu sits on the screen, so nothing is being recalled. No authority prescribes a number of top-level pages.
