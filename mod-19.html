Pick a lane

Why "any small business that needs a website" is the fastest way to stay broke, and how to choose the one trade you serve first.

Background

The answer that sounds ambitious and is not

Ask a beginner who their customer is and you will usually hear that they serve any small business needing a website. It sounds open-minded and it is actually the most limiting answer available.

Think about what that answer costs you in practice. Every pitch has to be built from scratch, every quote is a guess, every job teaches you something you will never use again, and nobody can refer you because nobody can describe what you do.

Now compare that with a person who says they build ordering sites for takeaway restaurants. That sentence does work for them while they sleep, because anybody who hears it knows exactly who to send.

Narrowing does not shrink your market, it shrinks the distance between you and the people already looking.

Who they are, and why they buy

Choosing a lane means understanding two separate things about a group of people, and most beginners only think about the first one.

Who they are is the countable part. What trade, how big, what part of town, how long in business, how many staff. You can gather all of it from a map and an afternoon.

Why they buy is the part that decides your pitch. What they are worried about at eleven at night, what they feel embarrassed about, what they believe about people like you, and what would make them feel foolish for saying yes.

A takeaway owner and a dentist might both need the same site technically, while wanting completely different things emotionally. The takeaway owner wants orders arriving without a phone call. The dentist wants to look established enough that a nervous patient trusts them.

Same product, entirely different sentence coming out of your mouth.

The mistake underneath the mistake Beginners avoid choosing because choosing feels like turning away money. In reality you are not turning anybody away, since you can still accept a job outside your lane whenever one arrives.

What choosing changes is where you point your attention, and attention is the only thing you actually have to spend.

Four lanes worth considering

These are not the only options, they are four that suit a person starting out with AI tools and no reputation.

Food and drink. Takeaways, cafes, food trucks, bakeries. Strong buying triggers, visible results, they talk to each other constantly, and ordering integrations create the expensive follow-on work. The downside is that owners are exhausted and hard to reach during service hours.

Trades. Plumbers, electricians, roofers, landscapers. They buy when the phone stops ringing, they understand paying for tools, and they rarely want to touch a website themselves. The downside is that they are on a roof when you call.

Personal services. Barbers, salons, nail studios, tattoo artists. Booking is the killer feature, appearance matters to them, and the community is tight and referral-heavy. The downside is thin margins, which caps the price.

Small clinics and practices. Dentists, chiropractors, therapists, opticians. They have real budgets and care about looking legitimate. The downside is slower decisions and, in some cases, privacy rules you would need to learn properly before promising anything.

What actually changes when you narrow

This is the part worth believing, because the benefits are larger than they sound.

  • Your pitch stops being generic. "I build websites" becomes "I get takeaway orders coming in after nine at night."
  • Your price goes up. Specialists charge more than generalists in every trade, and buyers accept it.
  • Your speed doubles. The second site in a trade takes half as long, because the questions and the structure repeat.
  • Your demo does the selling. One excellent example of their exact business beats any amount of explaining.
  • Referrals start working. People inside a trade know each other, and being known as the person for that trade compounds.
  • You learn faster. Ten jobs in one trade teaches you far more than ten jobs across ten trades.

That fourth point is the one to act on first, and the rest of this module builds toward it.

Answer the five questions

Module 03 gave you five questions about a customer type. Now you answer them properly, in writing, for the lane you are considering.

  1. What trade are they in, and how many exist within a fifteen-minute drive of you?
  2. Roughly how much does one customer spend with them in a single visit?
  3. How does a new customer currently find them?
  4. Which of the three buying triggers is most likely to apply to them?
  5. What would one extra customer a day be worth to them in a month?

Question five is the one that sets your price, and question one is the one that tells you whether the lane can feed you.

Here is the arithmetic that matters. If there are forty businesses of that type within a reasonable drive and you eventually serve eight of them on care plans, that is a real income from a tiny share of a small market. If there are six of them, the lane is too narrow and you should widen it to a neighboring trade rather than abandoning the idea.

Build the demo before you need it

This is the single highest-value thing in the module, and almost nobody does it.

Pick a real business in your chosen lane, one you are not currently trying to sell to, and build them an excellent site anyway. Take your time, make it genuinely good, and use their real information.

Then use that one site as your entire sales material for the whole trade. You are no longer describing what you could do, you are showing what you already did for somebody exactly like them.

Two practical notes. Tell the business you built it for them, because arriving with a free finished site is a strong opening rather than an awkward one. And build for a business slightly better than your typical target, since people compare upward.

Why the demo beats a portfolio A portfolio of nine sites across nine trades says you are available. One outstanding site in their exact trade says you understand their business.

A takeaway owner looking at another takeaway's site is not evaluating your design. They are picturing their own menu on that page, and that picture is what closes the sale.

When to add a second lane

Not yet, and there is a clear signal for when.

Add a second lane once the first one is either full or proven. Full means you have as many care clients from that trade as you can hold. Proven means you have closed at least three and can predict how the conversation goes.

When you do expand, move to a neighboring trade rather than a distant one. A person known for takeaway sites moves naturally to cafes and food trucks, since the buying trigger and the ordering problem are nearly identical.

Jumping from restaurants to law firms throws away everything you learned and starts you at zero with a worse story.

Do this before the next module

Choose one lane and write it down as a commitment rather than a preference.

Then count. Open a map, search that trade in your area, and list every business you find within a fifteen-minute drive. Stop at forty if there are more.

For each one, note whether they have a website, whether it works on a phone, and whether they can take an order or a booking online. You already ran this check on ten businesses in Module 03, so this is the same work at a larger scale.

That list is now your market, your research, and your prospect list all at once, and you will use it directly in the next module.

What you should have when you finishOne named lane, written down as a decision.

Written answers to all five questions, including what an extra daily customer is worth to that trade.

A counted list of every business in that trade near you, marked for website, phone, and ordering.

One demo site built for a real business in that lane, good enough to show anybody.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. Why is "any small business that needs a website" a limiting answer?

Every pitch is built from scratch, every quote is a guess, nothing you learn carries to the next job, and nobody can refer you because nobody can describe what you do. Narrowing shrinks the distance between you and the people already looking.

2. What is the difference between who they are and why they buy?

Who they are is countable — trade, size, location, years in business. Why they buy decides your pitch. A takeaway wants orders without a phone call; a dentist wants to look established enough that a nervous patient trusts them.

3. Why does one demo site beat a portfolio of nine?

A portfolio across nine trades says you are available. One outstanding site in their exact trade says you understand their business, and the owner looking at it is already picturing their own menu on that page.
