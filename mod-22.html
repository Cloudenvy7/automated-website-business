Background

Why a script is not dishonest

People resist scripts because they imagine sounding like a robot reading from a card, and that is a fair worry and it comes from bad scripts rather than from the idea itself.

A script is simply deciding what you think before somebody is looking at you. The alternative is improvising under pressure, and improvising under pressure is how prices get discounted, promises get made, and jobs get agreed that should never have been.

The owner standing in front of you has been telling their own story for years, and it comes out smoothly because they have said it several hundred times. You are allowed exactly the same advantage.

Learn the shape of it rather than the exact words, and when it starts sounding like you, that is when it is working.

The five parts of the conversation

Every sales conversation in this business has the same five parts, and they arrive in the same order every time.

  1. Open — say who you are and why you are there, in two sentences.
  2. Show — put the site in their hands and stop talking.
  3. Ask — find out what is actually costing them money.
  4. Price — give three options, say the numbers, stop talking again.
  5. Next step — agree one specific thing that happens next.

Most beginners do one, two and four, then leave, and the missing pieces are three and five, and they are where the sale actually lives.

Part 1: the open

Keep this to two sentences. Any longer than that and you are talking mainly to fill your own nerves.

"Hi, I am Marcus. I build websites for takeaways around here, and I noticed you did not have one, so I built you a first version to see what you thought."

Notice that there is no pitch in there, no credentials, and no claim about quality. There is a name, a specialty, and something that already exists.

Part 2: showing it

Hand them the phone and go quiet, and this is the hardest instruction in the module and the most valuable.

Every instinct you have will tell you to narrate what they are seeing. Do not explain your color choices, do not apologize for the placeholders, and do not fill the silence while they scroll.

Watch their face rather than watching your own work. You are looking for the exact moment their expression changes, because whatever is on the screen at that moment is the thing they actually care about, and it is what you will talk about for the rest of the conversation.

The most common beginner errorThe most common error is talking over the demo. The owner is trying to picture their own business inside what they are seeing, and every sentence you say interrupts that picture.

Count silently to twenty in your head if you have to, and let them speak first.

Part 3: asking

Once they have looked at it, you need to find out which problem is actually worth money to them, and three questions will do it.

  • "When somebody wants to order from you at nine at night, what happens right now?"
  • "How do most new customers find you?"
  • "If you could fix one thing about how people reach you, what would it be?"

Then listen properly and write down the exact words that they use. The answer to that third question is the sentence you will repeat back to them later when you give the price.

That piece of repetition matters far more than any argument you could construct. When you say "you told me people call after close and there is no way to order," you are not selling to them at all, you are quoting them.

Part 4: the price

Give them three options, said plainly, in about fifteen seconds altogether.

"There are three ways to do this. I can finish it and hand it over, and it is yours — that is fifteen hundred. I can finish it and keep it running for you, updating the menu and making sure it works, which is eight hundred to build and six hundred a month. Or I can add the ordering so people can pay through the site, which is around four thousand and takes a few weeks."

Then stop talking completely. The silence will feel unbearable to you and entirely normal to them.

Beginners lose more money in the four seconds after saying a price than they do anywhere else in this entire business, because they fill that gap with a discount which nobody actually asked for.

Part 5: the next step

Never leave a conversation without one specific thing agreed, and with an actual day attached to it.

Good next steps tend to sound something like these three:

  • "I will send the three options in writing tonight. Have a look and tell me which one fits by Friday."
  • "You said you need to talk to your brother. I will call you Thursday afternoon — does two work?"
  • "Send me your real menu and I will put it in properly, then you can see it as it would actually be."

That last one is the strongest move available to you when somebody is undecided, because it asks them for effort rather than money and it quietly gets them invested.

The six hard questions

These six come up constantly in this work, so rehearse them until they are genuinely boring to say.

"Can't I just do this myself with AI?"

Answer this one honestly, because the honest answer is considerably stronger than a defensive one.

"Yes, honestly. You could build something like this in an afternoon. Most owners I work with can too. What they find is that the building was never the hard part — it is the domain, the ordering connection, the Google listing, and remembering to update it in six months when you are busy. That is what you are actually paying me for."

Denying it makes you look either uninformed or dishonest, and they are going to find out anyway.

"Why is it so much?"

Do not try to justify it with hours, because hours are the weakest ground you could possibly stand on.

"Compared to what, is the fair question. If one extra order a night is worth thirty dollars, this pays for itself in about two months and then keeps going. If you do not think it will bring in an extra order a night, then you are right and you should not buy it."

That answer is genuinely fair to both of you, which is precisely why it works.

"Can you do it cheaper?"

"I can do less for less. The hand-it-over package is fifteen hundred and I do not maintain it. What I would rather not do is charge you less and quietly do a worse job, because you would not be happy and neither would I."

You have refused the discount without refusing the customer, and you offered them a real path at the same time.

"How do I know you will not disappear?"

This is the real fear sitting behind most hesitation, and it is especially strong for owners who have been burned before.

"Fair question, and you cannot know for certain. What I can do is put the domain in your name, not mine, so if I vanish tomorrow you still own everything. That is how I set every job up, and it is deliberately the opposite of how the person who burned you probably did it."

Then actually do that, because Module 05 and Module 09 make it the rule.

"The last person did it for free."

"They might have. What usually happens with free is that it stops getting updated, because nobody keeps doing free work forever. I charge monthly so it stays somebody's job."

"Let me think about it."

Almost always means one of three things: no money, no authority, or no urgency. Find out which, gently.

"Of course. Just so I know what to send you — is it the price, or the timing, or is there somebody else you want to run it past?"

Whichever they name, you now have a real conversation instead of a polite exit.

Trust is the actual product

The transcripts and surveys behind this book keep pointing toward one thing. Small business owners are not mainly worried about design or about price, they are worried about being taken advantage of by somebody who then disappears.

Four particular things build that trust faster than anything you could possibly say.

  • Put the domain in their name. It is the most concrete proof available to you that you are not holding them hostage in any way.
  • Do the free thing first. Fix their Google listing properly before they have paid you a single dollar.
  • Show up exactly when you said you would. Being on time twice is worth more than any promise you could make.
  • Say the price without flinching at all. People instinctively trust somebody who is comfortable with their own number.

None of those require you to be experienced, and they only require you to be deliberate, which is available on your very first conversation.

Do this before the next module

Write out your own version of all five parts, in your own words, on a single page.

Then say the whole thing out loud to a wall, twice over. After that, say it to a person who is not a customer, and ask them what they think it costs and what they think you do.

If they get either of those answers wrong, then your script is not clear enough yet, and the fix is nearly always fewer words rather than better ones.
