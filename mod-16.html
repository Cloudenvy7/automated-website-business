Scope, or the "can you also add" problem

How small free favors quietly eat a one-person business, and the exact words that turn a favor back into a quote.

Background

The moment it happens

You send across the finished site and the owner is genuinely delighted, and then comes the one sentence that decides whether this job actually made any money.

"This is great. Can you also connect it so people can order through the delivery apps?"

Nothing about that request is unreasonable in the slightest. From where the owner is standing it sounds like one more small thing, roughly the same size as changing a photo, because both requests take the same length of time to say out loud.

From where you are standing it is a completely different job. It may take two weeks, it touches another company's system, and it can break in ways that you cannot fix on your own.

That gap between how big a request sounds and how big it actually is has ended more small web businesses than any competitor ever has.

Why this hurts more than it looks

Say yes to that just once and three separate things happen at the same time.

The first one is obvious, which is that you work for free. The second is worse, because you have now taught the client that requests of that size are free, and every future request gets measured against it.

The third one is what nobody sees coming, which is that the unpaid job takes over the paid one. Your two-week free favor means the next client waits, the follow-ups never get sent, and the month ends with less money than the month before despite more hours worked.

The honest reason people say yes It is not weakness or poor business sense at all, it is that saying no feels like risking the relationship at the exact moment when it is going well.

The fix is not becoming a tougher person, the fix is having a sentence ready in advance, so that your answer is not something you improvise while the client is smiling at you.

The one sentence

Learn this sentence and then adapt it to your own voice, because it does the entire job on its own.

"Absolutely, that is something I can do. It is a separate piece of work though, so let me price it properly and send it over tomorrow."

Look carefully at what those three parts accomplish. You said yes, which protects the relationship, and you named it as separate work, which resets the size of the request. You also bought yourself a day, which stops you inventing a number under pressure.

You have not refused anything at all, and you have not agreed to do it for free either. Almost every scope conversation you will ever have can be handled with that sentence alone.

Telling the two kinds of request apart

You do need a rule for what counts as already included, otherwise you will apply that sentence to a photo swap and end up looking ridiculous.

Inside the work you already agreed:

  • Fixing something that you built yourself which does not actually work correctly.
  • Correcting a spelling error, a wrong price, or a set of opening hours that changed.
  • Swapping out one photograph and putting a different photograph in its place.
  • One single consolidated round of changes during the review step of the pipeline.

A separate job:

  • Anything at all that connects to another company's system, meaning ordering, delivery, booking, or payments.
  • A brand new page that did not exist anywhere in the plan you both agreed to.
  • Rewriting a whole section after that section had already been reviewed and approved.
  • Anything needing an account, a login, or a subscription that the client does not yet have.
  • Rescuing or repairing something that you did not build in the first place.

The dividing line here is not effort at all, it is category. A new connection is a different kind of thing from a corrected typo, even on the rare occasion when the typo takes longer.

Writing the job down before it starts

Most scope arguments are really memory arguments in disguise. Two people remember the same conversation quite differently, and the person with nothing written down is the one who loses.

Your protection here is a short paragraph rather than a legal document. Before any build begins at all, send something like this and get a clear yes back in writing:

The scope paragraph "To confirm what we agreed: a one-page site for King Sandwiches with a menu section, an about section, and contact details, on a domain you own. Includes one round of changes after you review it. Price is fifteen hundred dollars, half now and half at launch, finished within three weeks of getting your menu.

Anything outside that — online ordering, booking, extra pages — I am happy to do, and I will quote it separately."

That is six sentences. Notice the final one especially, because it pre-agrees the rule while there is still nothing to argue about. When the request arrives later, you are pointing at something they already accepted rather than introducing a new condition.

Change orders without paperwork

The phrase "change order" sounds like something from a construction contract, and for a one-person business it is really just a short message.

It has three parts: what they asked for, what it will cost, and what it does to the timeline.

"You asked for online ordering through the delivery apps. That is nine hundred dollars and about two weeks, and it would push the launch to the ninth. Reply yes and I will start Monday."

Send that message and then simply wait. Do not soften it, do not add an apology, and do not offer a discount just because you feel awkward. The client will either say yes, say not right now, or ask a question, and all three of those outcomes are perfectly fine.

When to give something away

Rules with no exceptions at all tend to get ignored, so here is the one honest exception.

Give away something small and visible when the job is nearly finished and the client has been good to work with. Fix the thing quietly, and then tell them what you did.

"I fixed the crooked photo on the menu page and tidied the spacing while I was in there. No charge, it was five minutes."

That builds enormous goodwill precisely because you named it as free rather than doing it silently, since silent free work only teaches the client that the work was never worth anything.

Never give away the big thing, meaning never give away the integration, the extra pages, or the rescue job. Those are exactly where your profit lives, and giving one away does not create loyalty at all, it creates an expectation.

The awkward three, rehearsed

These three come up constantly in this work, so practice them out loud before you actually need them.

"Can you just quickly..." "I can do that. It is quick for you to ask and about half a day of work for me, so let me send you a price and we can decide together."

"The last guy did that for free." "He might well have done. I price the small things into the monthly plan instead, which is exactly why that plan covers everything coming up during the month."

"I thought that was included." "I can see exactly why you would think that, because it was not written down clearly enough on my side. Here is what is included, and here is what this particular one costs. My fault on the wording."

That last one is worth understanding properly. You take responsibility for the ambiguity, which costs you nothing at all, and you still charge for the work. People accept a price far more readily when they are not simultaneously being told that they were wrong.

Do this before the next module

Write out your own scope paragraph as a template with blanks left in it, and then write your change-order message as a template as well.

Then look honestly at your own history, whether that means a job, a favor for family, or a side project. Find one occasion when you did work that should have been paid, and write a single sentence about the request that started it.

That sentence describes your own pattern. Most people have exactly one weak spot in this area, and knowing which one is yours is most of the defense.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. Say the one sentence that turns a favor back into a quote.

"Absolutely, that is something I can do. It is a separate piece of work though, so let me price it properly and send it over tomorrow." You said yes, you reset the size of the request, and you bought a day so you are not inventing a number under pressure.

2. What separates an included change from a separate job?

Category, not effort. Fixing your own work, correcting a price, swapping a photo and one review round are included. Anything connecting to another company's system, a new page, or a rescue job is separate — even on the rare occasion the typo takes longer.

3. When should you give work away, and when never?

Give away something small and visible near the end of a job, and say out loud that it was free. Never give away the integration, the extra pages, or the rescue — those are where your profit lives, and giving one away creates an expectation rather than loyalty.
