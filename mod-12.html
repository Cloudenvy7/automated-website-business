Write it down or you do not own it

Turning what you already do into an operations manual, so the business stops living inside your head.

Background

The promise three modules have already made

Module 01 said this business quietly collapses without checklists. Module 11 said the twenty-point list was the beginning of something larger. Module 13 said the folder you built was the start of a manual.

This is that module, and it is considerably shorter than you would expect, because most of the work has already been done.

You are not going to sit down and write a manual out of nothing at all. You are going to collect the things you have already made and put them in one place, arranged in an order that somebody else could actually follow.

Why this is the difference between a job and a business

Gerber's test is worth repeating here in its practical form. Could somebody else run this from your written instructions?

If the answer is no, then every hour the business runs is an hour you personally have to be present for, which means you cannot be ill and you cannot go away for a fortnight. You cannot ever sell the thing either, because there is nothing to sell except your own labor.

That is not some distant concern for somebody with three clients, because it shows up much sooner than that, in three fairly ordinary ways.

Quality drifts. The fifth site misses two checks the first one caught, because you were tired that Thursday. Things get forgotten. A deposit not taken, a renewal not tracked, a follow-up not sent. You cannot hand off the smallest task, so you keep doing the ten-dollar work at the same time as the thousand-dollar work.

A written system fixes all three of those at once, and it costs you a single afternoon to build.

What goes in it

Your manual has six sections, and you have already built most of them.

One: the pipeline. The seven steps from Module 13, along with the condition that has to be true before each one of them.

Two: the scripts. What you say on the first call, the quote wording, the review instructions, the launch message, the price sentences, and the six rehearsed answers from Module 22.

Three: the checklists. The seven-point draft check from Module 06 and the twenty-point pre-launch check from Module 11, both printable.

Four: the templates. The intake form, the quote, the scope paragraph, the change order, the invoice, and the care plan agreement.

Five: the how-to notes. Short written steps for the things you do rarely enough to forget: connecting a domain, setting up business email, adding an order button, claiming a Google listing.

Six: the client records. One folder for each client, holding the domain details, the renewal date, where the logins live, the brand files, and what they are currently paying.

That is the whole manual, and notice that five of those six already exist somewhere in your notes. This module is therefore mostly an act of gathering rather than writing.

The one rule for how it is written Write every instruction so that a careful person who has never done this could follow it on the first try.

Not a person as skilled as you. Not a person who "knows the basics." Somebody careful and completely new, which is who you will actually be handing it to, and it is also who you will be at seven in the morning eight months from now.

The fastest way to write it

Do not open a blank document and try to remember your own process, because you will produce something idealized and largely useless.

Record yourself doing the real work instead, and write it up from that.

  1. The next time you do a job, screen-record it. Any free tool. Talk out loud while you work, including the parts where you hesitate.
  2. Afterwards, watch it back at double speed and write down each thing you did as a numbered step.
  3. Mark every place you paused, because a pause is where a decision lives, and a decision needs a written rule.
  4. Cut it to the shortest version that still works. If a step needs a paragraph of explanation, the step is really two steps.
  5. Give it to somebody else and watch them try. Every place they stop is a gap in your writing rather than a failure of theirs.

Step five is the only real test in that list, because everything before it is simply you believing your own instructions.

Where to keep it

Keep it somewhere boring, and more importantly somewhere that you will actually open.

One folder, in whatever cloud storage you already use, containing six subfolders that match the six sections above. Nothing clever, no special software, and no system that needs maintaining for its own sake.

Two practical rules. The manual lives in the same place as the client folders, so you are never hunting in two systems. And the checklists must be printable, because a checklist you have to open a laptop to read will not get run on a Friday afternoon.

Keep it alive or it rots

A manual written once and then never touched becomes wrong within a few months, and a wrong manual is worse than no manual at all, because people follow it.

The habit that keeps it true is a very small one. On the day something goes wrong, update the manual before you fix anything else.

A client got upset because they did not know the launch date. Add a line to the quote template. An order button silently stopped working. Add a monthly check to the care plan list. You forgot which registrar somebody used. Add that field to the client record.

Every mistake is worth exactly one line, and after a year of that you have something no competitor can copy, because it is built from your own specific scars.

The trap for Manager-heavy readers If Module 04 told you that you lean Manager, this module is the one you will over-do. You will build a beautiful system with folders inside folders and spend three weekends on it.

Set a limit: one afternoon for version one, and it only contains things you have actually done at least once. A manual describing work you have never performed is fiction.

What this unlocks

Three things become possible the moment this manual exists, and not one of them is available to you before that.

You can take a week off. Whatever has been written down, somebody else is able to cover, even if that somebody is a friend following your notes.

You can hand off the bottom box. Module 24 says to give away work from the bottom of the chart upward, and the only reason that works is a written system. Handing off an undocumented job is abandonment.

You can sell the business. A buyer is not buying your talent at all, they are buying a machine with clients attached, and no manual means there is no machine.

Do this before the next module

Make the folder today, with all six subfolders, and put into it every single thing that you have already written across this book.

You will find that four of the six fill up almost immediately. Then pick out the emptiest section, which is usually the how-to notes, and write one procedure properly.

Choose the procedure you dread doing, because that dread is almost always a sign that you have never written it down and are reconstructing it from scratch every time.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. Name the six sections of the operations manual.

The pipeline, the scripts, the checklists, the templates, the how-to notes, and the client records. Five of the six already exist in your notes, which makes this mostly an act of gathering.

2. What is the fastest way to write a procedure down?

Screen-record yourself doing the real job, talk out loud including the hesitations, then watch it back and write each action as a numbered step. Every place you paused is a decision that needs a written rule.
