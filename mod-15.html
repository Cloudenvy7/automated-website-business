Getting paid and staying legal

Registering the business, separating the money, sending an invoice that gets paid, and what to do when it does not.

This module explains how small businesses in the United States usually handle these things, but it is not legal advice and it is not tax advice, and the person writing it is not your lawyer or your accountant.

Rules differ by state and they change over time. Once real money starts moving, an hour with an accountant costs less than most people expect and is worth considerably more than this page is.

With all that said, most of what follows is ordinary practice rather than anything complicated, and simply not knowing it is what keeps a lot of people stuck.

You are already a business

Here is the part that surprises people the most. The moment somebody pays you for work, you are operating a business in the eyes of the tax authorities, whether or not you ever registered anything.

That default arrangement is called a sole proprietorship, it costs nothing at all, it happens automatically, and the money simply counts as part of your income.

So the real question is never whether to be a business at all, it is whether to keep that default arrangement or to register something more formal instead.

Sole proprietor or LLC

An LLC, meaning a limited liability company, is the structure that most small operators eventually choose, and understanding what it actually does prevents both overthinking and carelessness.

What an LLC gives you. It separates your business from you personally, so that a claim against the business does not automatically reach your car and your savings, and that separation is the entire point rather than a technicality.

What an LLC does not give you. It does not reduce your taxes by itself, it does not make you look serious to customers who never ask, and it does not protect you if you keep everything in one bank account and treat the company as your wallet.

What it costs. Filing fees vary widely by state, running roughly fifty to five hundred dollars to start plus a yearly amount in many places. Your state's own business filing website is the correct place to do this, and it is usually a form taking about twenty minutes.

A reasonable rule for decidingStart as a sole proprietor while you are learning and doing your first jobs. Register an LLC once any of these becomes true: you are earning enough that the money matters, you are touching client payment systems, or a client asks you to sign something.

Doing it in that order is not laziness. It means you spend the fee once you know the business is real.

Separate the money on day one

This one matters considerably more than the LLC and it costs nothing at all, so do it even while you are still a sole proprietor.

Open a second bank account and run every single dollar of business money through it. Client payments go in, tool subscriptions and domains come out, and you pay yourself by moving money across to your personal account.

Three reasons, all practical:

  • Taxes become simple. One account statement becomes your entire record, instead of a year spent untangling everything.
  • You can see the truth. Mixed money hides whether the business is actually making anything or quietly losing it.
  • The LLC protection depends on it. Separation on paper means very little if the money itself was never separated in practice.

While you are there, set up a way to take card payments as well, because getting paid should never be harder for the client than reaching for their wallet.

The three documents

You need exactly three documents, and not one of them is complicated to produce.

The quote. What you will do, what it costs, when it is done, what happens next. One page, sent before any work starts, covered in Module 13.

The scope paragraph. What is included and what is separate, agreed in writing before you build. That is Module 16.

The invoice. The one document whose entire job is getting you paid.

An invoice needs your business name and contact details, the client's name, an invoice number, the date, a plain description of the work, the amount, how to pay, and when it is due. That is genuinely all of it. Any invoicing tool will produce one, and a well-formatted document will do in a pinch.

Terms that actually get you paid

The terms matter considerably more than the wording does, and small businesses get to set stricter terms than large ones do.

Take a deposit. Half the build fee before work begins, or all of it on anything under a thousand dollars. This is completely normal and nobody serious ever objects to it.

Invoice the balance on launch day. Send it the same day rather than next week, while the value is sitting right in front of them.

Say "due on receipt" rather than "net 30." Thirty-day terms are a convention borrowed from large companies with accounts payable departments. A local restaurant owner does not have one of those, and they pay from their phone the moment an invoice arrives.

Charge for the monthly plan automatically. Set up a recurring payment rather than sending out a monthly invoice, because invoices get forgotten and every forgotten one costs you an awkward conversation.

Set the tax money aside as it arrives

This is the place where new business owners most often get hurt, and it is entirely avoidable.

Nobody withholds tax from a client payment on your behalf. The whole amount arrives, feels like income, and then gets spent, and afterward a tax bill arrives for money that no longer exists.

The habit that prevents this is very simple. Every time a client payment lands, immediately move a portion of it into a separate savings account and then do not touch it.

Roughly a quarter to a third is the range people commonly set aside, and the right figure for you depends on your state, your other income, and your deductions. That is precisely the sort of question worth asking an accountant once, early, before the numbers get large.

Also keep every receipt for anything you buy for the business, because those costs generally reduce what you end up owing. Photograph them and drop them into one folder, which takes four seconds and is worth real money.

The quarterly thing nobody mentionsOnce you earn meaningfully on your own, the tax system generally expects payment during the year rather than all at once at the end. Missing that can add penalties on top of the bill.

This is not something to work out from a web page. Ask an accountant what applies to you, in your first year, before it becomes expensive.

When a client does not pay

This will happen eventually, and the outcome depends almost entirely on staying calm and getting in early.

Day one after due. Assume it was simply forgotten, because it usually was. "Hi, just floating the invoice from the ninth back up in case it got buried. Here it is again."

Day seven. Slightly firmer, still friendly. "Wanted to check this one is on your radar. Is there anything you need from me to get it processed?"

Day fourteen. Name the consequence plainly, without heat. "I have not been able to reach you on this invoice. I am going to pause work on the site until it is settled. Happy to pick straight back up once it is."

Day thirty. Decide whether it is worth continuing to chase. On a small amount, one more direct message and then writing it off is often cheaper than months of your own energy.

The best protection here is the one you already applied at step three, because a deposit means an unpaid client can only ever cost you half.

One thing you should never doDo not take the site down as leverage, or hold their domain because payment is late.

You may be within your rights depending on the agreement, and it will still follow you around a small business community for years. Pause future work instead, which is the professional move and hurts your reputation not at all.

Do this before the next module

There are three tasks here, and not one of them takes an entire afternoon.

  1. Open a separate bank account for business money, even if it holds twelve dollars today.
  2. Make your invoice template, with all eight items on it, saved and ready.
  3. Look up your own state's filing page and write down what an LLC costs there, and what the yearly fee is. Do not file yet if you are still learning, just know the number.

Then write one line in your notes stating the percentage you will set aside for tax out of every payment. Choosing that now, before any money is involved, is far easier than choosing it later on.

What you should have when you finishA separate business bank account, open and ready.

An invoice template you can fill in and send in two minutes.

Your state's LLC cost, written down, along with the yearly renewal figure.

A tax set-aside percentage, decided in advance and written where you will see it.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. When are you legally operating a business?

The moment somebody pays you for work, whether or not you registered anything. That default is a sole proprietorship, so the real question is never whether to be a business but whether to formalise it.

2. Why does the separate bank account matter more than the LLC?

It costs nothing, it makes tax simple, it lets you see whether the business is actually making money, and the LLC's protection depends on it anyway. Separation on paper means little if the money was never separated in practice.

3. An invoice is fourteen days overdue. What do you say, and what do you never do?

Name the consequence plainly and without heat: you are pausing work until it is settled, and happy to pick straight back up. Never take the site down or hold their domain as leverage — that follows you around a small business community for years.
