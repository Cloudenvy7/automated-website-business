Two real case studies

A business with no product yet, and a working restaurant with a Facebook page — what each one teaches about when to build and when to wait.

Why these two

Both are real conversations, and they sit at opposite ends of a spectrum you will meet constantly.

One is a person with enormous drive and no product to sell yet. The other is a working business, already serving customers daily, with no proper website at all.

The interesting part is that the second is a far better first client than the first, and most beginners would instinctively choose the wrong one.

Case one: the sandwich business that does not exist yet

The situation. A delivery driver in Seattle wants to bring a specific regional sandwich north, built around a bread he cannot yet make. He has real drive, knows the city intimately, and has three children to support. He does not have the recipe, a location, a license, or a single item to sell.

He asks for a website for the business.

What was built. A one-page site in about fifteen minutes: a main page, an about section, a menu with invented placeholder prices, and a generated image of a busy summer scene. A brand style guide followed, with a gold and blue palette and a crown over the letter K.

What went right. Three things, all worth copying.

The build itself proved the point instantly. Fifteen minutes from nothing to something visible changed the conversation from theoretical to real, and that shift is most of the value of building in front of somebody.

The style guide came before the logo argument, which meant the back-and-forth about the crown had a written reference to settle against rather than two people describing pictures in their heads.

And the honest pricing conversation happened early: roughly two hundred fifty dollars to hand it over, or around two thousand for something involving delivery integrations and a year of support. Naming both numbers before either was requested set the frame properly.

What went wrong, and it is worth naming. The site was built on the builder's own account, so the preview link carried somebody else's brand and the sharing settings had to be changed on the spot. A permission error appeared during the demo.

Larger than that: there was no product. A menu with invented prices, for food that cannot currently be made, launched on a real domain would be advertising something that does not exist.

The lessonA site for a business with no product is a planning tool, not a storefront.

Build it, share it as a preview, use it to think with. Do not connect a domain, do not publish it, and do not charge a launch fee for something that cannot yet take a customer.

What you can honestly charge for here is the brand work — the style guide, the logo, the name — because that is real and it survives whatever the business becomes.

What to do instead. With somebody in this position, the useful sequence is: brand first, preview site second, and launch only when there is something to sell. Then stay in touch, because a person with that much drive who does get their product together becomes an excellent client.

Case two: the chicken and waffle restaurant

The situation. A working restaurant with real customers, real food, and a Facebook page. No website. The person suggesting it eats there regularly and knows the owner, which is the strongest possible opening.

Why this is the better first client. Everything the first case was missing is already present. There is a real menu with real prices, real food to photograph, real hours, a real address, and customers who already exist and can be counted.

The buying trigger is obvious and undeniable. Search the name plus the city and see what appears. If the answer is a social page and nothing else, the gap can be shown on a phone in ten seconds.

How the job should run. Straight down the pipeline from Module 13.

Build first, before asking. Take the menu from their Facebook page, build a real site in forty minutes, and go in during the dead hours between two and four. Show it on a phone, and say nothing while they scroll.

Then the numbers conversation. If they are already on a delivery marketplace, they are paying somewhere between fifteen and thirty percent on those orders. Their own ordering page costs a processing fee of around three percent instead. On three hundred dollars a night that difference is roughly two thousand dollars a month, and that arithmetic is the entire pitch.

What to watch for. Two things specific to this kind of business.

Restaurant menus change more often than owners expect, so agree in writing who updates prices and how quickly. That single conversation prevents most maintenance arguments.

And accessibility matters more here than almost anywhere. Roughly a fifth of digital accessibility lawsuits in 2025 involved food service, and most defendants were small businesses. Build to the standard from the start, as Module 11 sets out.

The lessonThe best first client is a working business with an obvious gap and somebody who already trusts you.

Not the most exciting idea. Not the friend with the big vision. The place you already eat at, that already has customers, that cannot be found on a phone.

The uncomfortable general lesson

Enthusiasm is not the same as readiness, and beginners consistently confuse them.

The person with the big vision is exciting to talk to and gives you energy, while the restaurant owner is tired, distracted, and busy. The second one has money, a product, and a problem you can fix this week.

That does not mean abandoning the dreamer. It means being honest about what you can build for them today, charging for the part that is real, and putting your selling attention where the paid work actually is.

Module 23 has the sharper version of this. Excitement from somebody who has not paid you anything is the most reliable way to talk yourself into a bad plan.

What to take from bothBuild in front of people. Fifteen minutes changes a conversation more than an hour of description.

Brand before build, because it settles arguments that otherwise have no reference point.

Do not launch a storefront for a business with nothing to sell. Charge for the brand instead.

Choose the boring client first. Working business, obvious gap, somebody who already knows you.
