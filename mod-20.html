Background

What this tool is for

A business plan is a long document that nobody ever reads and that stops being true within about a month. The Business Model Canvas is a single page carrying nine boxes on it, and it does the genuinely useful part of that job in roughly twenty minutes.

The value here is not really the drawing itself. It is that those nine boxes force you to notice the parts of your business you have never thought about, which for most beginners turns out to be roughly five of them.

It comes from a book called Business Model Generation by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, and it has been used by companies of every size since 2010. It works just as well for one person with a laptop.

The nine boxes, in plain words

Read through these once, and then fill every one of them in for yourself.

1. Customer segments — who you serve. Not everybody who might conceivably buy something, but the one specific group that you named back in Module 19.

2. Value proposition — what you actually give them. Not "a website" at all, but the specific result that arrives in their business because of it.

3. Channels — how you reach them. Walking in during quiet hours, referrals from existing clients, the demo site you built, and a listing somewhere they already look.

4. Customer relationships — what kind of relationship it is. Either a one-off transaction that ends, or ongoing care that continues.

5. Revenue streams — how money arrives. Build fees at the start, monthly care plans afterward, and add-on projects whenever they are needed.

6. Key resources — what you must have. Your own hours, the AI tools you pay for, a phone, and the demo site that does your selling.

7. Key activities — what you actually spend your hours doing. Building, selling, maintaining, and learning, in whatever proportions are real.

8. Key partners — who you rely on. The AI provider, the domain registrar, the payment processor, and any subcontractor doing the hard connections.

9. Cost structure — where money goes out. The subscriptions, the yearly fees, and the cost of your own time, which is the one people forget.

The first five are the customer side and the last four are the machine that delivers it, and a business works when those two sides balance.

Filled in for this business

Here is the completed canvas for a one-person website shop serving takeaways, using the actual numbers from earlier modules.

If your week does not look roughly like that split, your business is not the one described on this canvas.

Where the money quietly leaks

The cost box is the place where one-person businesses most often fool themselves, because most of their real costs never arrive as bills.

Your unpaid time is the biggest cost of all. Every hour spent on an unquoted favor, an unpaid rescue, or a third round of revisions is money leaving the business, and it simply never appears on a statement anywhere.

Subscriptions accumulate quietly. Two tools quietly become five over a year, so check them every quarter and cancel whatever you stopped using.

Cheap clients cost you more than they actually pay. Module 14 covered why that happens, and on the canvas it shows up as an activity cost that the revenue box never manages to cover.

Unused capacity is a cost. If you can hold ten care clients and you currently have three, that gap is not neutral at all. It is seven clients of income you are not earning while your time passes anyway.

Changing one box changes the others

This is the real use of the canvas, and it is genuinely worth trying properly rather than skipping.

Pick any one box and change it on paper, and then trace carefully what happens to all the rest.

Example: change the customer segment from takeaways to dentists.

  • Value proposition changes from ordering to looking trustworthy.
  • Channels change, because you cannot walk into a dental practice during dead hours the same way.
  • Revenue goes up, since budgets are larger.
  • Key activities change, because there may be privacy rules to learn.
  • The relationship becomes slower and more formal.

Five boxes moved from one decision, and that is why choosing a lane is a strategy decision rather than a marketing one.

Example: drop the build fee to zero and charge only monthly.

  • Revenue in month one goes to nothing, so cash gets harder.
  • Customer relationships get stickier, because leaving costs them their site.
  • You need more savings to survive the gap.
  • The sales conversation gets easier, since the barrier to saying yes drops.

That is a real strategy some shops run, and the canvas shows you its cost before you find out the expensive way.

Do this before the next module

Draw nine boxes onto one sheet of paper. Actual paper works better here than a screen, because it quietly stops you polishing.

Fill in all nine for your own business as it exists today, not as you hope it will be. Where a box is empty or vague, that is not a failure of the exercise, it is the finding.

Then run one change experiment. Pick a single box, change what is written in it, and note down which of the other boxes move as a result. If nothing else moves at all, then you have not changed anything meaningful.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. What do the nine boxes split into?

The first five are the customer side — segments, value, channels, relationships, revenue. The last four are the machine that delivers it — resources, activities, partners, costs. A business works when the two sides balance.

2. Roughly what share of your week should selling take?

About forty percent, with building around a quarter. Almost every beginner has that backwards in their head and spends their time accordingly.

3. Name a cost that never arrives as a bill.

Your unpaid time — every unquoted favor, unpaid rescue and third revision round. Also unused capacity: if you can hold ten care clients and have three, that gap is seven clients of income passing you by.
