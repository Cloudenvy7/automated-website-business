Background

One person, three jobs, constant argument

In 1986 a man called Michael Gerber wrote a book about why most small businesses fail, and the central idea has held up remarkably well. He said that every small business owner is really three people at once, and that those three are permanently at war.

He named those three the Technician, the Manager and the Entrepreneur, and understanding which one you are at any given moment explains most of what actually happens inside a one-person business.

By his estimate the typical owner is about seventy percent Technician, twenty percent Manager, and ten percent Entrepreneur. That imbalance is not a personal failing, it is simply how people end up in business, and it is also why so many of them stay small or fold.

The Technician

The Technician is the doer of the three, and this is the personality that lives entirely inside today.

They love the feel of the work itself, and their creed is that if you want something done right you should do it yourself. They think about how, almost never about why, and to them thinking is unproductive unless it is thinking about the job in front of them.

In this business the Technician is the one who builds the site, meaning the domain, the hosting, the pages, the images, the menus, the ordering buttons, and the twenty-point check at the end.

Everything in Part 2 belongs to this role, and if that part was the easiest reading in the book so far, you are probably Technician-heavy.

The Manager

The Manager is the organizer of the three, and this is the personality that lives largely in the past.

They want order, predictability, and things that repeat reliably. Gerber's image for this is the person who buys plastic boxes and then arranges every screw in neat rows along the garage wall. Where the dreamer sees an opportunity arriving, the Manager sees all the problems that will come with it.

In this business the Manager decides the price, sends the invoice, runs the seven-step pipeline, and writes down what happened so it can happen again.

That is Part 3 of this book, and if pricing and paperwork felt like a chore rather than a relief, then this is probably your weakest role.

The Entrepreneur

The Entrepreneur is the dreamer of the three, and this is the personality that lives out in the future.

They turn small things into large opportunities, they create change, and they create a certain amount of chaos on the way. They are the reason the business exists at all, and they are usually the reason it suddenly changes direction.

In this business the Entrepreneur decides who you serve, what you charge, and what the company looks like when it is finished.

That is Part 4, and it is the role most people skip entirely because it never feels urgent.

The Fatal AssumptionGerber's sharpest idea is this. Most people who start a small business are not entrepreneurs at all, they are skilled workers who assumed something untrue.

The assumption is that if you understand the work of a business, you understand a business that does that work. Those are two completely different things, which is why a brilliant baker so often ends up with a failing bakery.

Applied here: being able to build a website teaches you nothing at all about running a website business, and this whole book exists because of that gap.

A short self-check

Answer these honestly rather than aspirationally, and for each pair simply pick whichever statement sounds more like you.

  1. (a) I would rather build something than plan it. (b) I would rather plan it properly first.
  2. (a) I keep most things in my head. (b) I write things down and keep lists.
  3. (a) I get restless doing the same job twice. (b) I like it when a job goes the same way every time.
  4. (a) I would rather do it myself than explain it. (b) I would rather write instructions once.
  5. (a) I often think about what this could become in a year. (b) I think mostly about this week.
  6. (a) Talking about money makes me uncomfortable. (b) I am fine naming a price.

Now count them up. Questions 1 and 4 answered (a) point toward the Technician, while questions 2, 3 and 6 answered (b) point toward the Manager. Question 5 answered (a) points toward the Entrepreneur.

Most people who reach this page score high on Technician, low on Manager, and somewhere in the middle on Entrepreneur. That is the ordinary result and it is worth knowing rather than fixing.

Working on it rather than in it

This is the single line that the whole book hangs from, and it is worth stating carefully rather than quickly.

Working in the business means doing the work: building the site, fixing the phone number, writing the menu. Working on the business means building the thing that does the work: the checklist, the price sheet, the pipeline, the script.

Gerber's own test for this is blunt. If the business depends on you, then you do not own a business at all, you own a job. It is also a demanding job, because you are working for somebody who has no boundaries whatsoever.

His practical trick is to imagine the business you are building is the prototype for five thousand more exactly like it. Not one more, and not ten, because those numbers still let you answer "I will just handle that myself." Five thousand forces every answer to be written down.

You are obviously not going to open five thousand web shops. The point of choosing such a large number is that it changes the question from "how do I do this" into "how would somebody else do this from my written instructions."

How to read the rest of this book

Use the result of your self-check to decide where in this book you should slow down.

  • Technician-heavy, which is most people. Part 2 will feel natural and you will be tempted to stop there. Force yourself through Part 3, because that is where the money is decided.
  • Manager-heavy. You will enjoy the checklists and may over-plan. Set a deadline for finishing Part 2 and actually build something before you perfect the system.
  • Entrepreneur-heavy. You will love Part 4 and skim the rest. Your risk is a beautiful plan with no delivered work behind it, so finish one real site before you design the company.

Nobody is genuinely balanced across all three, and balance is not really the goal here. The goal is knowing which hat you are wearing today, and noticing when you have been wearing the same one for a month.

Do this before the next module

Write down your three scores from the self-check above, and then answer one further question in writing.

Which role have I been avoiding, and what is the smallest thing I could do this week that belongs to it?

For most readers the honest answer involves either naming a price or talking to a stranger, and the smallest version of either one is enough to start.

Check yourself

Three questions, no score, nothing recorded. Answer each one in your head first, then reveal. Trying to recall something is what moves it into memory — rereading does not, which is why reading it again feels productive and is not.

1. Name the three roles and what each one does in this business.

The Technician builds the site. The Manager sets the price, sends the invoice and runs the pipeline. The Entrepreneur decides who you serve and what the company looks like when it is finished.

2. State the Fatal Assumption.

That if you understand the work of a business, you understand a business that does that work. Being able to build a website teaches you nothing about running a website business, which is the reason this whole book exists.

3. What is Gerber's test for whether you own a business?

If the business depends on you, you do not own a business, you own a job. The practical version is asking whether somebody else could run it from your written instructions.
