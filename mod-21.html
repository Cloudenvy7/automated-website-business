Finding customers when nobody knows you

The local sales motion for somebody with no list, no reputation, and no budget for advertising.

EntrepreneurFog index 8.658 min read

The only real problem you have

You can build a site in an afternoon and you have nobody at all to build it for. That is the whole difficulty in one sentence, and it is worth naming plainly, because beginners so often respond by studying the building even harder.

There is no clever solution here and there is a reliable one. You talk to local business owners, more of them than feels comfortable, in a structured way, and you keep track of who you talked to.

The numbers involved here are entirely ordinary. Expect somewhere around twenty real conversations to produce one paying client while you are starting out and unknown, and that ratio improves quickly once you have a lane and something to show, although it never becomes zero.

Knowing that ratio in advance changes how a refusal actually feels. A no is not a verdict on you personally, it is simply one of the nineteen you have to get through in order to reach the twentieth.

Build first, ask second

The old way was to ask for a meeting, describe what you could do for them, quote a price for it, and then hope. That approach always fights uphill, because the owner has to imagine every single part of it.

The modern way is available to you precisely because building became nearly free, and it is simply this: build the site first, and then go and show it to them.

Take a business from the list you made in Module 19, spend forty minutes building them a genuine site with their real menu and real photos, and then go and show them the thing that already exists.

The whole conversation changes shape after that. You are no longer a stranger asking for money in exchange for a promise, you are a person holding something they can see, and their only real decision is whether they want to keep it.

Two honest cautionsDo not use their logo or photographs in any way that suggests they endorsed it. Build the thing, show it to them, and make perfectly clear that it is a proposal. If they say no to it, then take it down.

Never publish it on a live domain before they have agreed to it. A preview link is a gift to them, while a published site they never asked for is a problem that you created.

Who to approach first

Work through your list in order of how obvious each problem is, because obvious problems tend to make for short conversations.

Tier one: no website at all. They have a social page and nothing else besides it, so the gap is completely undeniable and you can show them exactly what they are missing in about ten seconds.

Tier two: a site that fails on a phone. Pull it up in front of them and then stay completely quiet, because the argument makes itself without any help.

Tier three: a site that cannot take an order or a booking. This is the most valuable tier and the slowest sale, because the owner has to believe the lost revenue is real.

Tier four: everything else. Skip all of these entirely while you are starting out, because a business with a decent modern site is a hard first sale and a poor use of your first twenty conversations.

Working in that order also protects your confidence, since tier one conversations are the ones most likely to end well.

Walking in the door

For local businesses, turning up in person beats email substantially, and it is also the part that people dread most. Here is exactly how to make it survivable.

When to go. Never during their rush, under any circumstances. For food businesses, aim for the dead hours between two and four in the afternoon, and for trades try early morning or the very end of the day. For salons, choose mid-week rather than the weekend.

What to carry. Your phone with the site already open on it, and nothing else at all. No folder, no printed brochure, and no laptop, because you want this to feel like a conversation rather than a presentation.

What to say first. Something short, honest, and specific to their particular business.

The opening, word for word "Hi — I am not selling anything today, I promise. I build websites for takeaways around here, and I noticed you did not have one, so I built you a first version to see what you thought. Can I show you on my phone? It takes two minutes."

Then hand them the phone. Do not narrate it, do not explain your choices, and do not talk while they scroll.

When to leave. As soon as you have either shown them the site or established that they are busy, which is two to five minutes in total. Leaving early is a genuine strength, because it proves you respect their time and it makes the second visit easy.

Do not try to close anything on that first visit, because the entire goal of visit one is that they have seen the thing and now know your name.

What to do with the answers

You will get four different responses to this, and every one of them has a next move attached.

"This is great, how much?" Give them the three packages from Module 14, then say the price and stop talking.

"I do not have money for this right now." Believe them and stay friendly. "Totally understand. I will leave the link up for two weeks in case you want to show anyone. If it becomes useful later, you know where I am." Then note the date and come back in three months.

"I already have someone doing that." Ask one question rather than arguing. "Fair enough — are they looking after the ordering side too?" Roughly half the time the answer reveals a gap, and the other half you thank them and leave.

"Leave me your details." This usually means no, and it costs you nothing at all to be gracious about it. Leave them, note it down, and move on.

Turning one into three

Referrals are not really a separate skill at all, they are simply a question that you ask at the right moment.

The right moment is not at launch, when the client is being polite but has no evidence yet. It is roughly thirty days later, once something good has actually happened to their business.

Ask it specifically, because a vague request gets a vague answer. Do not say "let me know if you hear of anyone." Say this instead:

"Glad the orders are coming through. Who else around here do you think has the same problem you had? If you can think of two, I will go and see them."

Naming a number and naming the problem gives them something answerable, and one satisfied owner in a tight trade is worth more than a month of walking into doors cold.

Keep a list or you are just wandering

This is the unglamorous part of the work that separates the people who make it from the people who quietly quit.

Keep one simple written record. A spreadsheet is perfectly fine and so is a notebook, but your memory is not.

That is five columns and a date. Without it you will re-approach people you already saw, forget the person who said "come back in spring," and have no idea at all whether you are improving.

With it, you can answer the only question that matters: how many conversations did I have this week, and how many turned into next steps?

A realistic first month

Aim for five conversations a week, which is one a day on weekdays, and that comes to roughly twenty of them in a month.

Out of twenty conversations, expect roughly two or three real opportunities and perhaps one paying client. That single client is not the whole return either, because they also become your first referral source and your first real case study.

If you finish a month with zero conversations, then the problem is neither the market nor the tools. Everything else in this book is preparation for the one part that only works when you leave the house.

Do this before the next module

Take the list you built in Module 19 and mark every business on it with a tier from one to four.

Pick the top five from tier one, build a real site for the first of them, and then go and show it to them. After that, do the second one.

Track every visit in the five-column table, including the ones that went badly, because those are the ones that teach you what to say differently next time.
