Brand before build

Why the style guide comes first, and how to produce one in twenty minutes that protects you for the whole job.

TechnicianFog index 9.067 min read

The mistake that costs you an afternoon

Most beginners open the site builder, describe the business, and let the tool choose the colors for them. The result looks fine and the client says it looks fine, and then two days later the same client says the colors feel wrong and asks you to try something else entirely.

Now you are guessing, and guessing in front of a paying customer is both slow and expensive, because you will burn an entire afternoon generating options while the client changes their mind out loud.

The fix is to settle the look before you build anything, using a short document called a style guide, which takes about twenty minutes and removes almost all of that back and forth.

What a style guide actually is

A style guide is a short written description of how a brand looks, precise enough that a stranger could follow it and produce something matching.

A useful one for a small business contains six things:

  • The colors, given as exact codes rather than as words like blue, because the word blue means about forty different things.
  • The fonts, meaning one for the headings and one for the body text, along with the sizes for each.
  • The logo, described carefully in words, including the things it must never be allowed to do.
  • The tagline, which is a short phrase appearing near the name on signs and on the site.
  • The photography style, so that every image on the site looks like it came from the same place.
  • The voice, meaning whether this particular business sounds warm, sharp, formal, or funny.

That is the whole document, and it fits on one page. You are not writing this for a design agency, you are writing it so that you, the AI tool, and any future helper all produce the same thing.

The prompt that generates the first draft Make a one-page brand style guide for Rosa's Tamales, a family tamale business in Tacoma that sells at weekend markets and takes pickup orders.

The feeling should be warm, family-run, and proud, not corporate or fancy.

Include exact color codes, a heading font and a body font, a written description of the logo, three tagline options, a description of the photography style, and a short note on the voice.

Run that prompt, read what comes back, and change anything that feels wrong to you. The first draft is a starting point rather than an answer, and the whole value of it is that it gives you something specific to react to.

Choosing colors without a design background

You do not need taste for this so much as you need a rule, and the rule here is that a small business needs three colors and no more than that.

One main color carries the brand and appears on buttons and headings. One supporting color is used sparingly for emphasis. One neutral is the background, which should nearly always be white, off-white, or a very dark grey.

The most common beginner mistake is using two strong colors at equal weight, which makes a page feel loud and cheap, so pick one color to lead and let the other one appear only rarely.

There is one hard requirement, and it is not a matter of taste. Text has to be readable against its background, which means dark text on light backgrounds or light text on dark ones. Pale grey text on a white page fails for a large share of readers, and Module 11 covers why that failure has legal consequences now.

Getting the logo out of the owner's head

This is the part that frustrates people the most, because the owner usually has a very clear picture in mind and no words at all for describing it.

The trick is to ask about position, weight, and effect rather than asking whether they like it. Vague questions get vague answers, so use specific ones instead.

  • "Should the mark sit above the name, beside it, or on top of one of the letters?"
  • "Should the letters be thick and heavy, or thin and light?"
  • "Should the name be one solid color, or outlined, or have a shadow behind it?"
  • "If you saw this on a sign from across a parking lot, what would you want to notice first?"

Turn each answer into a plain instruction for the tool. A useful request sounds like this: put a small crown centered on top of the letter K, make the text gold, and add a thin blue outline around each letter.

Notice that the instruction names the object, the position, the color, and the effect, which is four pieces of information and no design vocabulary at all.

Change one thing at a time When you ask for five logo changes in a single message, you will usually get three of them and lose two silently. Ask for one change, look at the result, then ask for the next one. It feels slower and it is much faster.

Photography that does not look generated

The images an AI tool produces on the first pass often look slightly wrong in a way people notice without being able to explain.

Food comes out too glossy, hands look strange, and everything sits in a soft empty studio, so the fix is to describe a real scene with real conditions rather than describing a product.

Compare these two requests. Asking for "a photo of tamales" produces something plastic, while asking for "tamales being unwrapped on a scratched wooden table in daylight, steam rising, a hand holding one, background slightly out of focus" produces something believable.

Four details do most of the work here: the surface, the light, the motion, and whatever is happening at the edges of the frame. Name all four of them and the result improves dramatically.

There is one honest limit worth knowing. Generated food photography is good enough for a first version of a site, and real photographs of the actual food are always better. A sensible plan is to launch with generated images and offer a photo session later as a paid add-on.

Saving and handing over the files

This whole step takes about four minutes, and it separates you from every amateur the client has ever dealt with before.

Create one folder for the client and put five things in it:

  1. The style guide, saved as a document rather than left in a chat window.
  2. The logo, saved at large size with a transparent background if the tool offers it.
  3. A plain version of the logo in one color, for stamps, receipts, and signs.
  4. The color codes written in a text file, so nobody has to hunt for them.
  5. Any generated photographs you actually used on the site.

Then send that folder to the client and tell them plainly that these files belong to them. It costs you nothing at all, it builds real trust, and it prevents the awkward call two years later when somebody needs the logo for a menu board.

Do this before the next module

Take the business you built in Module 06 and produce a style guide for it now, working backward from the site you already made.

Then go looking for the mismatches, because there will certainly be some. The site probably uses four colors instead of three, or a heading font that does not appear anywhere in your new guide.

Fix the site to match the guide rather than fixing the guide to match the site. That order matters, because it teaches the habit you will need on every paid job from here forward.
