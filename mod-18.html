Your life first, then the business

Deciding what the business is actually for, and turning that into a number it has to hit.

EntrepreneurFog index 8.696 min read

Why this comes before strategy

Almost every business book you will pick up starts with the business itself, and Gerber deliberately starts somewhere else entirely.

His argument is that a business exists to serve the life of the person who owns it, so building one without deciding what your life is supposed to look like is how people end up successful and miserable at the same time. They built a machine that runs, and it runs over them.

This module asks you to write down two separate things: what you want your life to look like, and what the business must produce in order for that life to be possible.

The whole exercise takes about an hour and most people skip it entirely, which is exactly why so many one-person businesses end up drifting.

The five questions

Answer these in writing, in your own words, without editing yourself, and nobody else is reading this.

  1. What do I want my days to actually look like? Not the job title. The hours, who you see, where you are, what you do first in the morning.
  2. How much money do I need, and by when? A real number for a real month, not a fantasy and not a minimum.
  3. What do I want to be able to stop doing? This one is usually the most honest answer on the page.
  4. Who do I want to be able to take care of, and how? For many people this is the entire reason the business exists.
  5. When I am much older, what do I want to be able to say I did? Uncomfortable and useful.

Write real answers rather than tidy ones. "Be successful" is not an answer at all, while "be home when my kids get back from school, and not be worried about the car" certainly is.

A worked example "I want to work from home four days a week and be out of the house by three. I need four thousand five hundred a month after tax to be comfortable rather than surviving, and I want to stop driving for other people. I want my kids to see me running something rather than being run. And I want to be able to say I built something that was mine."

Specific, unglamorous, and entirely usable as a target.

Turning that into a number

Now comes the arithmetic, and it is mercifully short.

  1. Write down what you need monthly, after tax, from question two. Call it N.
  2. Add roughly a third for tax, since nothing is withheld for you. That is your gross monthly target, G.
  3. Take your monthly care plan price from Module 14. Call it P.
  4. Divide G by P. That is roughly how many care clients you need.

Work through an example. If you need four thousand five hundred a month to live on, then your gross target is about six thousand, and at six hundred a month per client that comes to ten care clients.

Ten. Not a hundred, not a thousand, and that is the entire business.

The number matters because it turns an unbounded ambition into something countable. You are no longer trying to build a successful company, you are trying to find ten businesses in one trade near you and look after them properly.

What "finished" looks like

Gerber calls this the strategic objective, which sounds grander than it is, and it is a written description of the business at the point where it is doing its job.

Yours should answer five specific things about the business:

  • How much money it makes, both monthly recurring and a rough yearly total.
  • How many clients that is, and in which trade.
  • How many hours a week it takes from you.
  • What your role in it is by then, since it may not be building sites.
  • When you expect to be there, as an actual date rather than "someday."

Write the whole thing as one short paragraph, and then put a real date on it.

A finished description, written out "By June 2028, this business looks after twelve takeaway and cafe websites in south Seattle on care plans averaging six hundred a month, which is around seven thousand two hundred monthly recurring plus two or three builds a year. It takes about twenty hours a week, and I do the client conversations and the pricing, and I subcontract the integration work. I am out of the car entirely."

That is a finished business, and it is also modest enough to be believable, which is what makes it useful.

Using the target to say no

Here is the practical payoff of all this, and it arrives considerably sooner than you would expect.

Once the target is written down, every opportunity gets measured against it rather than against your mood. Somebody offers you a one-off logo job, a website for a business three hours away, or a partnership that sounds exciting. You look at the paragraph and ask one question: does this move me toward twelve care clients in my trade, or away?

Most opportunities are away, and they are not bad, they are simply somebody else's business.

That is the real function of writing this down. Not motivation, which fades, but a standard that lets you refuse things without agonizing over each one.

The trap of the number being too big

One warning belongs here, because ambitious readers walk straight into it.

If your target requires forty clients, you have not designed a one-person business, you have designed a company with staff, an office, and management problems. That may be what you want, and it is a different book.

Check your own number against reality for a moment. Can one person genuinely look after that many relationships alongside everything else the business demands? For most people the honest ceiling sits somewhere between eight and fifteen care clients while working alone.

If your life requires more money than that produces, the answer is usually to raise the price rather than to add clients. Ten clients at nine hundred is a calmer business than twenty at four hundred and fifty, and it earns the same.

Do this before the next module

Write both pieces out properly, and then keep them somewhere you will actually see them.

Page one: your answers to the five questions, in your own words.

Page two: the finished description, one paragraph, with a date and real numbers in it.

Then do the arithmetic and write the client number at the top of page two in large writing. That number is the thing you are actually building, and every other module in this book is a method for reaching it.
