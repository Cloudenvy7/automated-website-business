Background

The difference between a draft and a website

Up to this point you have been sending preview links, and a preview link is simply not a website. It lives under your account, it carries somebody else's brand inside the address, and it can disappear entirely if your subscription lapses.

Going live means the site answers at a name that the business itself owns, and that is the moment the work becomes real. It is also, not coincidentally, the moment when you can reasonably ask to be paid.

This module covers that single transition and nothing else. It is the most technical thing in the whole book, and it still only takes about thirty minutes of actual work.

Step one: choosing the name

Get this settled before anybody spends any money, because changing a domain name afterward is genuinely painful for everybody involved.

Four rules cover almost every situation:

  • Use the business name whenever it is available, with no hyphens, no numbers, and nothing clever added to it.
  • Prefer .com unless the business is a nonprofit or a local government body, because customers still type .com out of habit.
  • Say it out loud over an imaginary phone call. If you find yourself having to spell it, then it is the wrong name.
  • Check that a very similar name is not already a business nearby, because that kind of confusion hurts both of you.

If the exact name is already taken, then add the city rather than adding a number to the end. Rosastamalestacoma reads like a real business, while rosastamales2 reads like an accident.

Step two: the client buys it

This is the rule from Module 05 and it applies with no exceptions, so here it is again in practice.

Sit with the owner or share a screen, and have them register the domain using their own card and their own email address. Talk them through the checkout carefully, because registrars push a whole stack of extras and most of those are unnecessary.

What to accept and what to decline:

  • Accept privacy protection, which hides their home address from public records and is usually free these days.
  • Accept auto-renewal, because an expired domain is the single worst preventable failure in this entire trade.
  • Decline the website builder bundle, since you are already building the site somewhere else entirely.
  • Decline the search engine submission package, because it does nothing that you cannot do for free.
  • Decide about business email separately, since it is genuinely useful and gets covered further down this page.

Write the details down immediately Registrar name, the account email, the renewal date, and the yearly cost. Put those four facts in the client folder while you are sitting there. A year from now, that note is the difference between a two-minute renewal and a two-hour rescue.

Step three: connect the domain to the site

Now you point the new name at the site you built, and the mechanics of it are far simpler than the reputation suggests.

Open the settings for your site inside the AI tool and find the section for a custom domain. Enter the domain the client just bought, and the tool will show you one or two records that need to be added at the registrar.

Then open the registrar in another tab, find the DNS settings, and add exactly what the tool showed you. You are copying values from one screen onto another screen, and nothing more than that.

Three things go wrong here often enough to expect them:

  1. A trailing dot or a missing one. Copy the value exactly as given, including any final period.
  2. Using the wrong record type. If the tool asks for a CNAME, do not add an A record instead.
  3. Leaving the registrar's default records in place. Old records pointing at a parking page will fight the new ones. Remove them.

Step four: wait, and manage the waiting

After you save those records, the change has to spread across the whole internet, and that spreading takes a certain amount of time.

It usually finishes within an hour, though it can take up to a full day. During that window the site may work for you and not for the client, or work on a phone and not on a laptop, which is normal and not a sign of failure.

Tell the client this before it happens rather than after. A sentence like "it will look inconsistent for a few hours while the change spreads, and I will confirm tomorrow morning" turns a worrying period into an expected one.

Do not keep making further changes while you wait for it, because changing the records repeatedly makes the wait longer rather than shorter.

Step five: email on their own name

This is the upsell that nobody ever asks for and almost everybody wants once they understand what it does.

An owner emailing customers from a free personal account looks like an individual, while that same person emailing from their own business name looks like a company. It costs six to eight dollars a month per person and takes about fifteen minutes to set up.

There are two warnings here, and both of them have burned people before now.

First, if the business already uses email at that domain, changing DNS carelessly can break it. Ask before you touch anything, and if email already exists, find out where it lives first.

Second, moving an owner off an address they have used for a decade is a real project rather than a quick task. Set the new address up alongside the old one and let them migrate slowly.

Step six: the launch check

Do not tell anybody that the site is live until it passes all seven of these checks, and run every one of them yourself rather than assuming.

  1. Type the domain with no www and confirm the site appears.
  2. Type it again with www and confirm the same thing happens.
  3. Check that the address bar shows a padlock, meaning the connection is secure.
  4. Open the site on a phone using mobile data rather than the office wifi.
  5. Tap the phone number and confirm that it actually starts a call.
  6. Tap the address and confirm it opens a map to the correct place.
  7. Send the link to somebody uninvolved and ask them to open it cold.

That last check catches the errors your own eyes have long since stopped seeing. A person who has never looked at the page before will spot a misspelled street name in about four seconds.

The launch message to the client "Your site is live at rosastamales.com. I checked it on a phone and a laptop, the phone number dials, and the address opens a map. The domain is registered in your name and renews on March 14th. Here is the folder with your logo and colors."

Short, specific, and it quietly proves you did the work.

What you still owe them after launch

Launch day is not the end of the job, and being clear about that fact is what protects you afterward.

If you sold a one-time build, then hand over the files, confirm they can reach the site settings, and say plainly that future changes are separate work at a stated price. If you sold a care plan, then this is the day the monthly clock starts, and Module 14 covers what belongs inside that plan.

Either way, say it out loud on launch day itself. Almost every unpaid support relationship in this trade began with a launch where nobody ever defined what came next.

Do this before the next module

Take the practice site from Module 06 and put it on a real domain you own.

Buy a cheap name, connect it properly, and then run the full seven-point launch check on it. Doing this once with your own money and nobody watching means that the first time you do it for a paying client, you will already know exactly what every screen looks like.
