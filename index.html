Part A — the idea behind the whole thingOne person has to play three roles

Michael Gerber's The E-Myth says every small business owner is really three people in one, and those three people fight each other. Most owners are about 70% Technician, 20% Manager, and 10% Entrepreneur. That mix is why most small businesses stay small or die. This playbook gives each role its own section, so you can tell which hat you are wearing and when to switch.

Role 1

The Technician

The doer. Lives in today. Loves the feel of the work. His rule is "if you want it done right, do it yourself." He asks how, never why.

In this businessYou build the site. Domain, hosting, pages, images, menus, ordering buttons.

Role 2

The Manager

The organizer. Lives in the past. Wants order and things that repeat. He cleans up the mess the dreamer makes. Where the dreamer sees a chance, he sees a problem.

In this businessYou turn the build into a checklist anyone could follow. You set the price, send the invoice, hold the deadline.

Role 3

The Entrepreneur

The dreamer. Lives in the future. Turns small things into big opportunities. Creates change, and creates chaos along the way.

In this businessYou decide who you serve, what you charge, and what the company looks like when it is finished.
The line the whole playbook hangs on

Gerber's test: if the business depends on you, you do not own a business — you own a job. Every module ends by asking the same thing: could someone else do this from your written instructions?

Part B — the market, three snapshotsTwenty years of this same business

People selling websites to local businesses have existed for over 25 years. What they sell has changed three times. If you know the pattern, you can see where the money is going next instead of chasing where it used to be. Numbers below are real and sourced at the bottom of this page.

2006

Skill was the moat

  • 42% of US adults had home internet fast enough to matter. A website was a brochure, not a store.
  • Internet Explorer held 82–86% of browsers. Making a page look right took real skill.
  • The main tool, Dreamweaver 8, cost $399. You hand-wrote code and moved files by FTP.
  • The government did not even have a job code for "web developer" until 2010.
  • Around 35% of small firms had a site at all (2002 baseline).

2016

Templates ate the low end

  • US web design services was a $24.4B industry (2015), with no firm holding even 5% of it.
  • WordPress ran 27% of all websites. Wix had 97 million sign-ups and 2.5 million paying.
  • 95% of freelancers surveyed charged under $5,000 a site. Only 1% cleared $10,000.
  • 70% already sold a monthly plan — but only $25–$100 a month.
  • About half of US small businesses still had no website at all.

2026

AI ate the build itself

  • US web design services is now roughly $47B, but industry jobs have fallen about 4.8% a year.
  • 83% of US small businesses already have a site. The easy first sale is mostly gone.
  • AI cut the cost of a simple site by 60–85%. Nearly a quarter of AI builds now quote under $500.
  • Shops with no monthly income lose money 58% of the time. Cross 25% recurring and that drops under 10%.
  • Yet under 20% of solo designers' income is recurring, and 66% gross under $50k a year.
What the three snapshots add up to

In 2006 you got paid for knowing how. In 2016 you got paid for doing it faster than the client could. In 2026 the build is close to free, so you get paid for what happens after launch — the ordering button, the Google listing, the monthly care, the phone you answer. This playbook is built around that shift, not around building sites.
